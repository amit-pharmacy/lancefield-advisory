Pre-pack administration

The phrase describes an administration where the deal selling an insolvent company's trade and property is struck in advance, then signed the moment an administrator is in office. The company goes into administration, the insolvency practitioner appointed as administrator completes the pre-pack sale, and the business comes out the other side, usually the same day, owned by someone else. Sometimes that someone else is a trade buyer. Very often it is the existing directors, buying through a new company, the NewCo you will see referred to in every guide.

The point of doing it this way is value. The worth of most small businesses sits in things that evaporate the moment the word administration becomes public: customers, contracts, suppliers, staff, goodwill. A pre-pack sale moves the business assets across before that happens, and with them the jobs and the relationships with the stakeholders who matter: customers, suppliers, landlord, staff. The criticism is also about value: unsecured creditors find out afterwards, get little or nothing, and watch the same people carry on trading from the same premises. Both things are true, which is why the rules around pre-pack administration have tightened over the last decade and why the decision deserves more care than it usually gets.

Is a pre-pack administration actually the right answer?

No one at Lancefield takes insolvency appointments, and we are not paid to find you a buyer. Since nothing we earn depends on a pre-pack administration going ahead, this question comes first rather than last.

A pre-pack administration suits a particular set of facts, and it is worth being honest about whether yours are among them.

It suits you if

The underlying business is viable and the problem is the insolvent company's balance sheet. There is a credible buyer with real money, whether that is a third party or you. The value of the business would be destroyed by a public insolvency procedure: contracts that terminate on insolvency, customers who would walk, staff who would leave. And the secured lender, if there is one, can be brought onside.

It does not suit you if

The trade itself is what has failed, in which case a new owner simply inherits the problem. Nobody can fund the purchase. The creditors would accept a repayment plan, which makes a company voluntary arrangement the better route because the company survives intact. Or the real aim is simply to leave the debts behind, which a pre-pack administration is not designed for and which the rules are specifically built to catch.

If a pre-pack administration is being proposed to you and nobody has asked whether a CVA, a turnaround or a straightforward liquidation would serve you better, ask. The answer should be reasoned, not assumed. The directors of the insolvent company are the people who will live with it.

How a pre-pack administration works

This is still an administration under the Insolvency Act 1986, so the insolvency practitioner taking the appointment carries the same duties as in any other. They answer to the creditors as a body, and they work through the statutory objectives in sequence: save the company if it can be saved, unless that is not realistically achievable or the creditors as a whole would do better from a sale, in which case the aim becomes beating what a straight winding up would have produced. Our administration page sets those objectives out in full. What marks a pre-pack out is when the deal gets done. In outline:

  1. Advice and viability.The directors take advice, usually from an insolvency practitioner and often through a contingency planning exercise, on whether the business is viable and whether a sale is realistic. In the Insolvency Act 1986 framework this is the stage at which the directors' own exposure is at its most manageable
  2. Marketing and valuation.An independent valuer puts a market value on the business and its assets, and the business is marketed, or the administrator records why it was not. That valuation is the part that later has to stand up to scrutiny
  3. The buyer and the terms.A buyer is identified, the price and terms are negotiated, and the funds are confirmed. If the buyer is connected to the company, the additional rules below apply
  4. Consents.A secured lender with a fixed charge has to release its security for those assets to be sold, or the court has to sanction the sale over it, and a lender holding a qualifying floating charge has its own right to appoint an administrator, so lenders are consulted before anything is signed
  5. Appointment.The administrator is appointed, usually out of court on the directors' or company's application, or by the floating charge holder. A Scottish company files in the Court of Session or the sheriff court. Notice then goes to Companies House and into the Gazette, which is the point at which the insolvent company's position becomes public
  6. Completion.The pre-pack sale completes, typically on the day of appointment or immediately after. Staff, assets and contracts move to the buyer. The old company is left with its liabilities, and with the unsecured creditors who hold them, and whatever was paid for the business
  7. Disclosure to creditors.The insolvency practitioner has to explain the pre-pack sale to creditors in a formal SIP 16 statement, the professional standard the regulators enforce,, covering the marketing, the valuations, the price and who the buyer is, with the first notification to creditors and normally within seven calendar days of the sale
  8. The rest of the administration.The directors provide a statement of affairs under the Insolvency Act 1986, the administrator sends proposals to creditors within eight weeks of appointment, collects in the sale proceeds and any other assets, reports on the directors' conduct, and in due course the old company usually moves into a creditors' voluntary liquidation or is dissolved. Where a creditors' voluntary liquidation follows, the liquidator completes the distribution to unsecured creditors

The administration itself lasts up to a year, extendable. The pre-pack sale, which is what everyone means by the pre-pack, is over in hours.

What transfers, and what stays behind

Directors sometimes picture a pre-pack administration as the company changing hands. It is not. The company stays where it is, in administration. What moves is the business: a defined list of business assets sold by the administrator to the buyer for a price.

Typically that list includes the goodwill and trading name, the customer contracts and order book that can be assigned, the intellectual property, meaning the trade marks, software, designs and know-how the business runs on, the website and domain names, the stock, the plant and equipment, the fixtures and the lease if the landlord agrees, and the employees, who transfer by law. Each of those assets is valued, and the total is the price the administrator has to justify as the best reasonably obtainable for the assets sold.

What stays behind in the old company is everything else: the unsecured debts, the tax liabilities, the pension obligations, any claims against the company, and the directors' loan accounts. The buyer is not taking on the company's liabilities unless the sale agreement says so, and the old company's creditors have no claim against the new one. That separation is the entire mechanism of a pre-pack administration, and it is why the rules insist on a fair price for the assets and full transparency with creditors.

Pre-pack or a trading administration?

In a trading administration the administrator is appointed first and then runs the business while looking for a buyer. It is more transparent, because the marketing happens under the insolvency practitioner's control and creditors and other stakeholders can see it. It is also more expensive and riskier, because the business has to survive being visibly in administration while the process runs. Trading administrations suit larger businesses with enough cash and enough goodwill to withstand it. A pre-pack administration suits the business that would not, where business continuity is the only thing protecting the value.

The administrator's job is to pick the route that produces the better outcome for creditors as a whole, and to be able to show why. Directors do not get to choose a pre-pack administration because it is more convenient for them, and a proposed administrator who behaves as if they do is a warning sign.

Buying the business back yourself

A large share of pre-pack administrations sell the business and its assets to the existing directors through a NewCo. This is lawful and often the best outcome available, because management is frequently the only buyer who understands the business, can move fast, and will keep the staff. It is also the version of a pre-pack administration that creditors most resent, and the rules have been written with that in mind. If you are contemplating it, these are the things that will be examined.

The price.
The administrator has to achieve full value for the assets and be able to evidence it. An independent valuer's view of market value is the floor. If a third party would have paid more for the assets, that is a problem for the administrator and, in practice, for you
The evaluator's report.
Since 2021, a sale of the business or a substantial part of its assets to a connected person within the first eight weeks of an administration cannot go ahead unless the creditors have approved it or the buyer has obtained a written report from an independent evaluator on whether the case for the sale is reasonable. The evaluator is chosen by the buyer, and must be independent, professionally insured and satisfied they have the knowledge and experience for the job. The report goes to the creditors with the SIP 16 statement, though the administrator may withhold anything they judge confidential or commercially sensitive, which is the point of the transparency the rules demand. A report saying the case is not made does not stop the sale, but it is disclosed alongside the administrator's written reasons for going ahead anyway, and it follows you. The voluntary Pre-Pack Pool that reviewed connected sales before 2021 has been superseded by this requirement, and now offers evaluator reports itself, with no special status under the regulations
Real money.
Deferred consideration is common, but the administrator has to be satisfied it will actually be paid, and will usually want security for it. A purchase funded by the cash you took out of the old company invites exactly the scrutiny you do not want
The company name.
The Insolvency Act 1986 restricts re-using the name of a company that has gone into insolvent liquidation, and breaching the restriction is a criminal offence that can make you personally answerable for the debts of the new company. There is a specific exception, but only where the business is bought from the insolvent company under arrangements made by the office-holder, not where the directors sold it to themselves beforehand. It needs formal notice to every creditor and an advert in the Edinburgh Gazette, given before you act under the old name. Get this right before you trade, not after
The old debts.
Every liability stays with the old company. So do your personal guarantees, and an overdrawn director's loan account, and anything the administrator identifies as a gratuitous alienation or an unfair preference in the run-up, These are the Scottish challenges, and their tests are not the same as the English ones: an unfair preference needs no proof that you wanted to prefer anyone, and on a gratuitous alienation it is for the person defending it to show the company was still solvent afterwards or that full value was paid. Where you were the one who received the value, the claim is pursued against you personally. A pre-pack administration does not launder the past

Done properly, a management pre-pack administration is a legitimate rescue with a full paper trail and SIP 16 transparency. Done badly, it is the case study the regulators have in mind. The difference is preparation, and it is why the work before the appointment matters more than the day itself.

What happens to employees

In most pre-pack administrations the staff transfer to the buyer with the business and its assets under the TUPE regulations, with their continuity of service intact, because TUPE treats the sale of a business as a transfer of the employees who work in it. That is one of the main reasons the procedure exists, and for employees it is the best outcome an insolvency procedure offers. Not every employee transfers: where the buyer is taking part of the business, or genuinely needs fewer people, the administrator makes the redundancies and those employees claim their statutory entitlements through the Redundancy Payments Service in the usual way. TUPE also has specific provisions for transfers out of insolvency under the Insolvency Act 1986 procedures, including which pre-transfer debts the National Insurance Fund meets rather than the buyer, and a buyer who does not understand TUPE before the sale tends to find out about it afterwards.

The thing to get right is communication. Staff usually learn of a pre-pack on the day it completes, which is unavoidable, but what they hear on that day, and from whom, shapes whether the business you have just bought still has a workforce on Monday.

What creditors get, and why they are angry

It is worth understanding the creditors' position, because you will be dealing with their reaction.

The price paid for the business assets goes into the old company, along with anything else the administrator realises from the assets left behind, and is distributed in the statutory order: the fixed-charge lender from the assets it holds security over, then the costs of the administration, then preferential creditors, employees' wage and holiday claims first and HMRC behind them as a secondary preferential creditor for VAT and PAYE, then a prescribed part set aside for unsecured creditors, then the floating charge holder, and finally the unsecured creditors at large. In most pre-packs the unsecured creditors receive little, and HMRC, usually among the largest creditors even after its preferential slice, notices. They find this out after the sale has completed, from a statement written by the administrator, and they see the same business trading on. Their anger is rational.

What they are entitled to is transparency, and SIP 16 exists to deliver it. That disclosure has to tell them how the business was marketed, what it was valued at, who bought it, what was paid, whether the buyer was connected, and why the administrator concluded the sale was the best available outcome. That transparency is the price of being allowed to do the sale without asking them first. Creditors can question the administrator, and if they believe the process was misused they can complain through the Insolvency Service's complaints gateway, which passes the complaint to the administrator's regulator. A well-run pre-pack survives that scrutiny. It is the thin ones that do not.

Suppliers you intend to keep working with, and any other stakeholders whose cooperation the NewCo needs, deserve a phone call, not a statutory notice. Whether they trade with the NewCo is up to them, and how you handle that conversation decides it.

What it means for you as a director

Every duty the Insolvency Act 1986 puts on directors of an insolvent company in an ordinary administration applies here too: the conduct report, wrongful trading, disqualification, an overdrawn loan account, and personal guarantees that the sale leaves completely untouched. Our administration page sets all of that out, and it is worth reading alongside this one. Three things, though, bite harder in a pre-pack administration.

The investigation is sharper when you are also the buyer.
The insolvency practitioner writes the conduct report knowing you now own the business, so any assets that left the insolvent company in the months beforehand get a closer look than they otherwise would, and so does the valuation the sale was priced on
The timing question is unavoidable.
A pre-pack administration is usually arranged over weeks while the company is already insolvent. That period is precisely the window wrongful trading liability is concerned with under the Insolvency Act 1986, so the advice you took and the records you kept during it matter more here than in an administration that arrives suddenly
Your guarantees follow you into the new company.
The old lender or landlord will still call on them, and the NewCo needs its own facilities and its own lease, frequently guaranteed again. Directors end up personally exposed on both sides at once, a liability best identified before the sale rather than after. Our Debt Help Scotland section deals with the personal half

None of this should put an honest director off a rescue that is genuinely the best outcome available. It should put them off doing it without advice.

What it costs, and how long it takes

A pre-pack administration is more expensive than a liquidation and cheaper than a trading administration. The costs are the administrator's fees, the valuers and agents, the legal work on the sale, and the statutory disbursements, and they are met first from what the buyer pays for the assets and from the old company's other assets. Where the buyer is management, the purchase price and the costs of the process are, in practice, both being found by the same people, which is why funding is the first practical question.

No figure appears here, for two reasons: we do not set these fees, and they end up in the SIP 16 disclosure the administrator makes to creditors, where an invented number would be worse than none. The spread between a simple asset sale and a contested business with property and lenders is too wide for a single answer anyway. Ask us and we will find out what the practitioner expects it to cost before you commit to anything.

On timing, the sale itself can complete within days of the appointment being decided upon, and the whole exercise from first advice to completion is often measured in weeks. The administration that follows runs for up to twelve months unless extended, but by then the business has moved on and your involvement is the director's statutory cooperation rather than the running of a process.

Pre-pack administration in Scotland

The connected-person regulations of 2021 and SIP 16 both reach Scotland, so the scrutiny a management buyer faces is identical. What changes is the machinery around it. A Scottish company's administration is run under the Scottish rules, not the England and Wales ones; the filings go to the Court of Session, or to the local sheriff court where paid-up share capital is £120,000 or less; advertisement is in the Edinburgh Gazette; and the excepted case that lets you keep using the old trading name has its own Scottish rule, though the 28-day deadline is the same.

None of that changes whether a pre-pack administration is the right answer, but it does mean the insolvency practitioner and the solicitors involved should be people who run Scottish administrations routinely. Working out of Glasgow, we deal with them week in, week out.
