Insolvency

Winding up petitions and compulsory liquidation

Compulsory liquidation is the closure of a company by court order, following a petition from someone the company owes money to. It is the one insolvency procedure that is done to a company rather than chosen by it.

Get urgent confidential advice

If a winding-up petition has been presented against your company, time is now the thing that matters most. The window in which you can still influence the outcome is short, and it effectively closes once the petition is advertised.

A winding up petition is most often presented by HMRC over unpaid VAT, PAYE or Corporation Tax, and by trade creditors and landlords over unpaid invoices and rent. Whether you see it written as a winding up petition or a winding-up petition, it means the same thing.

If you have just been served, read this first

Three things are true immediately, and knowing them changes what you do today.

Your bank account will probably be frozen

Once the petition is advertised in the Gazette, banks routinely freeze company accounts. Any payment made after the petition is presented can later be void, so banks protect themselves by stopping everything. Wages, suppliers and rent all stop with it.

Payments you make now can be reversed

Disposals of company property after the presentation of a petition can be set aside unless the court validates them. Paying a favoured creditor at this point can create a personal problem for you later, not just a company one.

Advertisement is the point of no return

In Scotland the court grants a first order that directs intimation, service and advertisement of the petition together, so there is no fixed waiting period between being served and the petition appearing in public. This is one of the places where English guidance will actively mislead you: south of the border there is a set gap, and here there is not. Whatever time you have is a matter of how quickly the order is granted and acted on, which is precisely why the day you are served is the day to take advice. Once the petition is advertised the account freezes, other creditors can support it, and stopping it becomes much harder.

So the single most useful thing you can do is take advice before advertisement, not after. A winding up petition dealt with in week one has options that the same winding up petition does not have in week four.

It is worth being clear about what a winding-up petition actually is, because the language around it is used loosely. It is not a demand and it is not a warning. It is a court action asking the court to end the company's existence and hand its assets to a liquidator to distribute among the creditors. The petitioning creditor is not seeking payment at that point, it is seeking the company's closure, and any payment it receives comes out of that process rather than instead of it. Directors who read a petition as a stronger version of a debt letter tend to respond too slowly, which is the most common and most expensive mistake we see.

How the process runs

The chain is fairly predictable, which is what makes early intervention possible.

  1. Statutory demand.A creditor owed more than £750 serves a formal demand. The company then has three weeks to pay, or to secure or compound the debt if the creditor will accept that. Let that pass without paying and the company is deemed unable to pay its debts under section 123 of the Insolvency Act 1986, which is what opens the door to a petition. A creditor does not have to start here, though. In Scotland, letting the days of charge run out on a charge for payment following a decree has the same effect, and a creditor who can simply satisfy the court that the company cannot pay its debts can petition without serving any demand at all, which is why a petition sometimes arrives with no warning shot
  2. Petition presented.If the demand is unmet, the creditor presents a petition to the court to wind up the company, paying the court dues. HMRC is the most common petitioning creditor
  3. Service.Notice of the petition is served at the registered office
  4. Advertisement.For a Scottish company the petition is advertised in the Edinburgh Gazette, and in whatever newspaper the court specifies. Banks act on this. Other creditors can now join as supporting creditors
  5. Petition hearing.The court hears the petition. The company can oppose it, seek an adjournment, or offer terms. Many companies instruct solicitors at this stage
  6. Winding-up order.If the court makes the order, the company is wound up compulsorily. Directors' powers cease immediately
  7. Interim liquidator.In Scotland the court appoints an interim liquidator at the moment it makes the order, and only a licensed insolvency practitioner can take that office. The creditors can choose a different liquidator afterwards

The liquidator then realises assets, agrees creditor claims, distributes any funds in the statutory order of priority under the Insolvency Act 1986, and reports on the conduct of each director. The company is eventually dissolved and removed from the register at Companies House.

How this differs in Scotland

Almost every guide to winding-up petitions online describes the procedure for England and Wales, referring to the High Court and the Companies Court, and to the Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016. That is not how it works here.

In Scotland, a winding-up petition is presented to the Court of Session in Edinburgh, or, where the company's paid-up share capital is £120,000 or less, to the Sheriff Court for the sheriffdom in which its registered office sits. Procedure, terminology and timetables follow Scottish court practice, and notices appear in the Edinburgh Gazette. Enforcement in Scotland is called diligence, not execution, and covers steps such as arrestment and attachment rather than the England and Wales equivalents.

The underlying insolvency legislation is largely common across Great Britain, but the court route, the local practice and the practical timetable are not. If you are being advised by someone working from English precedent, they are working from the wrong map.

What you can still do about it

The options narrow as the process advances, which is exactly why the timing matters.

Pay, or agree terms

If the debt is genuinely owed and the money can be found, paying it or agreeing terms with the petitioning creditor is the cleanest answer. The petitioner can then withdraw or consent to dismissal. Bear in mind that other creditors may have already joined by then.

Dispute the debt

A winding up petition is not a debt recovery tool for disputed debts, and the courts take a dim view of it being used as one. If the debt is genuinely and substantially disputed on real grounds, that is a strong basis to have the petition dismissed, and potentially to recover costs. What matters is that the dispute is real and evidenced: a genuine dispute about whether the work was done, or about what was agreed, is a defence, whereas simply not having the money is not.

Propose a CVA

If the business is viable underneath the debt, a company voluntary arrangement can deal with the whole creditor body rather than just the petitioner. This needs to start early, because building a proposal takes time the petition timetable may not allow.

Enter administration

Administration brings a statutory moratorium that stops creditor action, including the petition, where a rescue or a better outcome for creditors than winding up is realistically achievable. One timing point matters: once a petition has been presented, the quick out-of-court appointment route closes, and administration has to be applied for at court. Where a sale is lined up in advance, that becomes a pre-pack administration.

Choose a CVL instead

Where the company cannot be saved, a directors' own creditors' voluntary liquidation is generally a better outcome than waiting for a compulsory order. A creditors' voluntary liquidation is more orderly, you have some choice over the insolvency practitioner appointed, and conduct handled proactively tends to be viewed more favourably. Once a winding-up petition has been advertised, that choice starts to disappear.

When the petitioner is HMRC

A large share of winding up petitions in Scotland are presented by HMRC, and a petition from HMRC behaves differently from one brought by a trade creditor. HMRC will usually have written several times before it gets to this, so by the time a winding-up petition lands there is rarely any surprise about the debt itself.

Two things follow from that. HMRC is generally willing to discuss a Time to Pay arrangement, but its appetite falls away sharply once a petition has been presented, and further once it has been advertised. And because HMRC is frequently the largest single unsecured creditor, its position tends to decide whether any rescue proposal succeeds. A CVA that HMRC will not support is not a realistic plan, whatever the other creditors think of it.

If your debts are largely tax debts, the practical route is usually to engage before the petition, not after. That is advisory work, and it does not need an insolvency practitioner appointment to start.

What it costs

A creditor pays court dues to present a winding-up petition, and in practice has to find an insolvency practitioner willing to act as interim liquidator, which is part of why petitions tend to arrive only after other attempts to recover the debt have failed. The deposit English guidance quotes is security for the Official Receiver's fee and does not apply in Scotland. If the order is granted, those costs usually rank as an expense of the liquidation and are paid from whatever the company's assets realise.

For the company, the costs that matter are rarely the court's. Once the petition is advertised the bank account freezes, trading effectively stops, and the value of the business falls quickly. That is the real cost of waiting, and it is why acting in the first days is worth more than any argument you might make later. Directors sometimes ask whether it is cheaper to let the compulsory liquidation run than to fund a CVL. Occasionally it is. More often the loss of control costs considerably more than the fee saved.

What it means for directors

A compulsory winding up brings a mandatory report on the conduct of every director, made by the liquidator to the Secretary of State through the Insolvency Service.

Disqualification.
Where a company is wound up by the court and a director is found unfit to be involved in running a company, the court must disqualify them for between two and fifteen years. Many cases are settled by an undertaking given to the Secretary of State rather than at a hearing
Wrongful trading
exposure arises where trading continued past the point at which there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency, and personal contribution can be ordered
Personal guarantees
are unaffected by the liquidation and remain enforceable against you personally
Overdrawn director's loan accounts
will be pursued by the liquidator as an asset of the company
Reuse of the company name
is restricted, with criminal and personal liability consequences for getting it wrong

None of this is meant to alarm. Ordinary business failure, honestly handled and dealt with early, rarely produces these outcomes. Ignoring a petition for six weeks is what produces them.

If you are the creditor

For completeness, because some readers arrive from the other side. A winding-up petition is a powerful remedy, and a blunt one. Your petition requires court dues up front and, in practice, an insolvency practitioner prepared to act as interim liquidator, and it is appropriate where a debt is undisputed, substantial and unpaid, and where you are content for the company to be closed and to share whatever is realised with all other unsecured creditors.

It is not a good tool for a disputed debt, and using it that way can result in the petition being dismissed with a court order for costs against you. Most creditors instruct solicitors before issuing. Winding-up petitions also frequently produce a lower recovery than a negotiated settlement, because unsecured creditors typically receive very little in a compulsory liquidation, and the reputation damage to a customer you might otherwise trade with again is permanent. Take advice on whether it is really the best route to your money.
