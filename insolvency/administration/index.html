Insolvency

Company administration

In company administration a licensed insolvency practitioner takes control of a company as its administrator, under a formal insolvency procedure, and runs it, sells it or winds it down under the protection of a statutory moratorium that stops creditor action while they do. It exists to produce a better outcome than an immediate winding up: sometimes by rescuing the company itself, more often by selling the business as a going concern or realising its assets in a more orderly way.

Is company administration the right answer for you?

This firm takes no insolvency appointments, so nobody here stands to be appointed over your company and we earn nothing by recommending the procedure. It means we can tell you when a cheaper or simpler route does the job, which is why the question leads.

It suits you if

There is a viable business inside the insolvent company, and it needs protection from creditor action while a sale, a restructuring or a repayment proposal is put together. The assets are worth more kept together as a going concern, goodwill included, than sold piecemeal. A creditor is about to petition, a landlord is about to irritate the lease, or a lender is about to enforce, and a short breathing space would change the outcome. And there is funding, from the business, a buyer or a lender, to pay for it.

It does not suit you if

The trade has stopped working and there is nothing to sell, in which case a creditors' voluntary liquidation reaches the same end at a fraction of the cost. The creditors would agree a company voluntary arrangement without any need for protection, which keeps you in control and the business intact. Or there is no money to fund it: administration is expensive, and a business with no assets and no buyer cannot pay for it.

It is a tool for protecting value while something is done with it. If nothing is going to be done, the protection is pointless and the cost is real. A turnaround review before any appointment is the cheapest way to find out which side of that line you are on, and the business rescue options are wider the earlier it happens.

What it protects you from, and what it does not

The moratorium is the reason the procedure exists, so it is worth being exact about it. Once the company is in administration no resolution can be passed to wind it up at all, and no winding up order can be made against it apart from on a petition brought in the public interest or by the financial regulators. A creditor's petition can still be presented, but it cannot be taken through to an order.

Beyond that, and to a large extent from the moment the directors file notice of their intention to appoint where the company has given a lender a qualifying floating charge, a creditor cannot do any of the following without the administrator's consent or the court's permission:

  • Enforce security over the company's property, which is the restriction secured creditors feel most. No administrative receiver can be appointed at all
  • Repossess goods held under hire purchase, leasing or retention of title
  • Irritate a lease, or in England and Wales forfeit it by peaceable re-entry
  • Start or continue legal action, diligence, execution or other legal process against the company

Suppliers are also restricted, since 2020, from terminating supply contracts simply because a customer has entered an insolvency procedure, which matters to any business that needs to keep trading. In plain terms, the phone stops ringing. Creditor pressure that has consumed the directors' attention for months transfers to the administrator on the day of appointment.

What it does not do is equally important. It does not cancel a single debt. It does not protect you personally: a personal guarantee can still be called, and the creditor holding it can still pursue you. It does not stop a lender with a qualifying floating charge from appointing an administrator of its own choosing. And it does not last: this is a temporary state with a statutory clock on it.

The administrator's job

Administrators are licensed insolvency practitioners and officers of the court. The administrator acts in the interests of the creditors as a whole rather than the directors or any single creditor, and the Insolvency Act 1986 sets three objectives to be worked through in order. Saving the company itself, as a trading business, comes first. Where that cannot realistically be done, or where the creditors as a whole would do better the other way, the aim becomes a return to the general body of creditors that beats what winding the company up straight away would have produced. Only where neither is achievable does the job reduce to selling what is there and paying out the creditors holding security or preferential status.

In practice the first is rarely achieved, because by the time most companies reach this point the corporate entity is not worth saving even where the trade underneath it is. The second is where most administrations live, and the usual way of achieving it is a sale of the business and assets, either after a period of trading or as a pre-packaged sale agreed before the appointment. The realisation of what is left then follows.

From appointment, the directors' powers are suspended. They remain directors, they must cooperate and provide a statement of affairs, but they cannot exercise management powers without the administrator's consent. This is the single biggest difference from a voluntary arrangement, where the directors stay in control, and it is the point most directors have not grasped when they ask for administration.

Who can put a company into administration, and how

The company or its directors
out of court. Where the company has given a lender a qualifying floating charge, that means filing a notice of intention to appoint, which triggers an interim moratorium, and then a notice of appointment within ten business days. Where there is no such lender there is no notice of intention and no interim protection, only the notice of appointment. This is the most common route for an owner-managed company
A lender holding a qualifying floating charge
out of court, usually after the directors have given it notice or after a default. The lender chooses the administrator, and its right of appointment takes priority over the directors' choice
By court order
on the application of the company, its directors or a creditor, where an out-of-court appointment is not available, for example because a winding up petition has already been presented

The out-of-court routes are not open in every case. A company that has recently been in administration, that has a petition outstanding, or that has an administrative receiver in office, may need a court order. For a Scottish company the papers are lodged in the Court of Session, or in the sheriff court for the sheriffdom of the registered office where paid-up share capital is £120,000 or less. A winding up petition that has already been served changes the route and shortens the time, which is why that page tells you to act on the day you receive it.

Directors sometimes file a notice of intention purely to obtain the interim protection, with no real intention of appointing. The courts have taken a dim view of that, and so should you: it is a step to take only when an appointment is genuinely in prospect.

How the process runs

  1. Advice and preparation.The directors take advice on whether this is the right route, whether there is a buyer or a rescue plan, and how it will be funded. Where there is time, this is the contingency planning stage, and it is where the outcome is largely decided
  2. Appointment.By one of the routes above. Notice goes to Companies House, to every creditor and into the Gazette
  3. The protection takes effect.Creditor action stops. The administrator takes control of the company's affairs, property and bank accounts
  4. Statement of affairs.The directors provide a statement of the company's assets and liabilities, as a statutory declaration, within eleven days of the administrator asking for it
  5. Trading, sale or closure decision.The administrator decides whether the business can trade on, whether it should be sold and to whom, or whether it should be closed and asset realisation begun. A pre-pack sale completes at this point if one was arranged
  6. Proposals to creditors.Within eight weeks of the company entering administration the administrator sends the registrar, every creditor and every shareholder a statement of proposals setting out how the objectives will be achieved, and asks the creditors to decide whether to approve them, by a decision procedure or by deemed consent, within ten weeks of the appointment
  7. Implementation.The administrator trades, sells, collects in assets, deals with employees and suppliers, reports on the directors' conduct, and keeps creditors informed with progress reports
  8. Exit.The procedure ends automatically after twelve months unless the creditors or the court extend it. It ends with the company handed back to its directors rescued, or with a voluntary arrangement, or by moving into a creditors' voluntary liquidation, which is how the remaining realisation reaches unsecured creditors because an administrator cannot generally distribute to them without the court's permission, or by dissolution where there is nothing left to distribute

The honest summary is that company administration is usually a twelve-month corridor between a failing company and either a sale or a liquidation. What happens in the first few weeks decides which.

What it means for employees

On appointment, employment continues. The administrator has a short window, fourteen days, in which to decide which staff to keep, and those kept on after that point carry priority for their wages from then on. Where the business is sold, the employees who work in it transfer to the buyer with their continuity of service intact under the TUPE regulations. Where the business, or part of it, is closed, redundancies are made and the affected employees claim what they are owed, within the statutory limits, from the Redundancy Payments Service.

Staff almost always learn about it on the day it happens. What they hear, and from whom, shapes whether the business the administrator is trying to sell still has a workforce the following week.

What it means for you as a director

You lose control.
The administrator runs the company. You remain a director, you must cooperate, hand over the books and records and provide a statement of affairs, but you do not manage the business unless the administrator asks you to
Your conduct is investigated.
The administrator has a statutory duty to review the conduct of every director and report to the Insolvency Service. This happens in every case; it is not a sign that anything is wrong
Wrongful trading applies.
If you carried on past the point where insolvent liquidation or administration could no longer realistically be avoided, and creditors ended up worse off, the court can order you to contribute to the company's assets, usually measured by the extra loss creditors suffered over that period. Your defence is that once you knew there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency you took every step you ought to have taken to minimise creditors' losses, which is why the advice you took and the record of it matter
Personal guarantees survive.
The protection covers the company, not you. A lender or landlord holding your guarantee can call it, and that becomes a personal matter with its own answers in our Debt Help Scotland section
An overdrawn loan account is a debt you owe.
The administrator will pursue it
You can be the buyer.
A business owner often buys the business back through a new company, usually by pre-pack. That is lawful, and heavily regulated; the pre-pack page explains the rules

None of this should put an honest director off an administration that is genuinely the best outcome. It should put them off entering one without understanding it.

What it costs, and who pays

This is the most expensive of the formal procedures. The administrator's fees, the legal costs, the agents and valuers, and the costs of any trading all rank ahead of the creditors and come out of the realisation of the company's assets, and trading losses can add to them. Where a secured creditor has appointed, it often funds the process against its security. Where a pre-packaged sale is planned, the buyer's price is usually what funds it.

We do not quote fees here because we do not set them, and because a two-week pre-pack of a single site and a six-month trading administration of a multi-site business are not the same thing. What we will do is tell you what the insolvency practitioners we introduce expect it to cost, and whether a winding up would achieve the same result for less. The creditors' committee fixes the administrator's outlays and remuneration, or the creditors do it by a decision procedure where there is no committee, and the court fixes it if they do not. Creditors are sent the administrator's accounts and remuneration claim with the progress reports, and creditors holding a quarter of the debt by value can ask the court to reduce it, provided they apply within eight weeks of the end of the relevant accounting period.

Administration compared with the alternatives

Administration or CVA

A voluntary arrangement keeps the directors in control and the company intact, and binds creditors to a repayment plan. It has no protection of its own, so it suits a company that is not under immediate attack. Administration suits a company that needs protection first and a plan second, and the two are sometimes combined.

Administration or liquidation

A winding up ends the company. Administration may end it too, but after a period in which the business could be sold or traded. If there is nothing to sell and nothing to protect, a CVL is quicker, cheaper and reaches the same place; compulsory liquidations, where a creditor petitions, reach it on someone else's timetable. Our liquidation page sets out the whole family.

Administration or pre-pack

A pre-pack is a type of administration, not an alternative to it: the sale is agreed before the appointment and completed immediately after. It suits a business that would not survive a visible period in the procedure. A trading administration suits one that would.

Administration or receivership

A receiver is appointed by one lender and deals only with the property charged to it. In England and Wales that is usually an LPA receiver over a single property; in Scotland a heritable creditor enforces its standard security instead, and a floating charge created since 15 September 2003 cannot normally be used to appoint an administrative receiver, outside a short list of statutory exceptions for capital markets and project finance. Administration deals with the whole company, and once it begins a lender needs consent to enforce and the administrator can require a receiver to step aside. See LPA receivership.

Since 2020 there has also been a standalone moratorium, separate from administration, in which the directors stay in control while a monitor oversees a short breathing space of twenty business days, extendable. It is not the same thing as administration, and it fits far fewer companies than it sounds. A monitor has to be willing to say that rescuing the company as a going concern is likely, and throughout the moratorium the company must keep paying its rent for the period, its wages, any redundancy payments, anything supplied to it during the moratorium, the monitor's fees and its bank debt. A company that has been through another insolvency procedure in the previous twelve months cannot use it at all.

Administration in Scotland

The procedure is governed by the Insolvency Act 1986 across Great Britain, and the protection, the objectives and the twelve-month period are the same for a Scottish company. The detail differs. A company registered in Scotland makes its filings in the Court of Session, or in the sheriff court where paid-up share capital is £120,000 or less, follows the Scottish administration rules rather than the England and Wales rules, and advertises in the Edinburgh Gazette. The Scottish law on transactions that can be challenged, gratuitous alienations and unfair preferences, has its own tests.

None of that changes whether administration is the right answer, but it does mean the insolvency practitioners and solicitors involved should run Scottish cases routinely. Ours are Glasgow people who do.
