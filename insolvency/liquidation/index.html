Insolvency

Company liquidation, explained plainly

When a limited company is liquidated, everything it owns is turned into cash, that cash goes to the people it owes, and the company itself ceases to exist. Companies House removes it from the register and, in all but rare cases, that is the end of it.

That is the mechanical answer. The one you probably want is this: liquidation is not always the right answer, and it is not always necessary. Before you go any further, it is worth knowing whether your company needs to be liquidated at all.

First, the question most people skip

Directors usually arrive at liquidation having already decided it is the only option left. Often it is. Sometimes it is not.

If the business is still viable underneath the debt, a company voluntary arrangement or a turnaround plan may keep it trading. If the pressure is coming from one creditor rather than the whole book, there may be a narrower fix through debt restructuring. And if the company is solvent and you simply want to close it, you want a members' voluntary liquidation, which is a completely different process with a very different tax outcome.

We do not take appointments. No one here becomes your liquidator, so we earn nothing by telling you to liquidate. That is the point of asking us first.

The three types of liquidation

Which one applies depends on whether the company can pay its debts, and on who starts the process.

Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL)

The most common route for an insolvent company. The directors start it voluntarily, shareholders pass a resolution, and a licensed insolvency practitioner is appointed as liquidator to wind the company up in an orderly way. Choosing a CVL rather than waiting to be forced into liquidation generally reflects better on the directors' conduct.

Read more about CVL

Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL)

For solvent companies only. If the company can pay its debts in full and the directors can swear a declaration of solvency, an MVL closes it and returns the remaining funds to shareholders in a tax-efficient way. This is a positive process, usually chosen at retirement, after a sale, or when tidying up a group.

Read more about MVL

Compulsory liquidation

This one is done to you rather than by you. A creditor who is owed money petitions the court, and if that winding up petition succeeds the court orders the company to be wound up. In Scotland there is no Official Receiver: the court appoints an interim liquidator at the moment it makes the winding-up order, and the creditors can choose a different liquidator afterwards. By the time a winding up petition has been advertised, your bank account is usually frozen and your options have narrowed sharply. That is exactly why acting earlier matters.

Read more about compulsory liquidation

A solvent limited company that simply has no further use has its own route, and it is worth knowing that closing one is a different exercise entirely from closing an insolvent one.

There is also company dissolution, or strike off, which is not liquidation at all. It removes a dormant or unused company from the register without a formal winding up. It is only appropriate where there are no significant debts and no creditor is likely to object. Where a company has assets to distribute, an MVL is almost always the better route.

Not sure which applies? Compare CVL, MVL and compulsory liquidation side by side, or speak to us and we will tell you in one conversation.

Solvent or insolvent? That one question decides everything

Before anything else, establish which side of the line your limited company sits on, because it determines which type of company liquidation is even available to you.

A company is solvent for these purposes if its directors, having made a full inquiry into its affairs, can swear a statutory declaration that it will pay its debts in full with interest within a period they specify. That period cannot exceed twelve months from the start of the winding up. Twelve months is the ceiling, not an automatic term, and swearing the declaration without reasonable grounds is a criminal offence, so it is not a formality. Where that declaration can properly be made you can liquidate through an MVL and take what is left as capital. A company is insolvent if it cannot pay its debts as they fall due, or if what it owes comes to more than everything it owns. Once a company is insolvent, or close to it, or an insolvent liquidation or administration becomes probable, the directors' duties shift: creditors' interests have to be weighed alongside the shareholders', and the deeper the trouble the more weight they carry, until at the point insolvent liquidation is inevitable they come first.

That shift matters more than most directors realise. Trading on while insolvent, taking deposits you cannot fulfil or credit you cannot repay, is what creates personal exposure. The moment you suspect the company is insolvent is the moment to take advice, not the moment to press on and hope.

Solvent

Assets exceed liabilities, and the directors can declare the debts will be paid in full with interest within a stated period of up to twelve months. Route: MVL. Outcome: funds returned to shareholders, often with a favourable tax treatment.

Insolvent

Cannot pay debts as they fall due, or liabilities exceed assets. Route: CVL if you act, compulsory liquidation if a creditor acts first. Outcome: assets realised, proceeds distributed to creditors by statutory priority.

If you are genuinely unsure which applies, that is exactly the question a short diagnostic conversation settles.

What creditors can do while you are deciding

The clock is not neutral. While you weigh options, creditors have their own remedies, and the further those actions progress the fewer routes remain open to you.

Chasing and default notices
the informal stage, where most situations are still fully recoverable
Statutory demand
a formal demand for an undisputed debt, giving 21 days to pay before further steps
Court actions for the debt
producing a judgment that can then be enforced. In Scotland enforcement is called diligence and includes arrestment of bank accounts and earnings
Winding up petition
an application to the courts to wind up the company. Once a winding up petition is advertised, your bank account is likely to be frozen and other creditors can join
Compulsory liquidation
if the courts grant the winding-up order. In a compulsory winding up in Scotland the court appoints an interim liquidator when it makes the order, and the creditors can choose a different licensed practitioner afterwards

Each of these steps narrows what is possible. A company that talks to an insolvency practitioner at the default notice stage has options. The same limited company facing an advertised winding up petition has very few. Nothing about that sequence is unusual or shameful, but it is worth understanding that delay is not a neutral choice, it is a decision to let creditors set the pace.

What actually happens in a liquidation

Once a liquidator is appointed, they take control of the company. Directors' powers cease, though your duties to cooperate do not.

  • The insolvency practitioner appointed as liquidator takes charge of the company's assets and its bank account
  • Assets are valued and sold, and the proceeds collected
  • The company's liabilities are established, including contingent liabilities that may not yet have crystallised, such as leases and guarantees
  • Employees are made redundant, and can claim statutory payments through the Redundancy Payments Service
  • Creditors are invited to submit claims and are paid in the statutory order of priority set out in the Insolvency Act 1986
  • The liquidator investigates the conduct of each director, which is a required part of the job rather than a sign of suspicion
  • The company is dissolved and removed from the register

Secured creditors are paid first from the assets they hold security over, then preferential creditors including employees for certain claims, then HMRC for specific taxes, and finally unsecured creditors. In most insolvent liquidations, unsecured creditors receive little or nothing. Shareholders come last and usually receive nothing at all.

What it means for you as a director

This is the part that keeps people awake, and it deserves a straight answer.

In most cases, liquidating a company does not make a director personally liable for its debts. The limited company structure exists precisely to separate the two, so the company's liabilities stay with the company. There are exceptions, and they matter. An insolvency practitioner acting as liquidator has a statutory duty to review how each director behaved in the period before insolvency, so it is worth understanding where the lines fall.

Personal guarantees

If you signed a personal guarantee, for a bank facility, a lease, or a supplier account, that guarantee survives the liquidation. The creditor can pursue you personally for the amount guaranteed. This is the single most common unpleasant surprise we see, and it is worth establishing exactly what you have signed before anything else happens.

Overdrawn director's loan account

If you have drawn more from the company than you put in, the liquidator will treat the balance as an asset of the company and ask you to repay it.

Wrongful trading

If you continued to trade and take on credit after the point where you knew, or ought to have known, that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency, a court can order you to contribute personally. Acting early is the clearest defence available to you.

Director disqualification

The liquidator reports on the conduct of every director. Where conduct has fallen short, a disqualification order can follow, for up to fifteen years, preventing you from acting as a director of any company. Ordinary business failure, honestly handled, does not usually lead to this. Directors who took advice early and kept records are in a considerably better position than those who did not.

What it costs, and what if you cannot afford it

A liquidator's fees are normally paid from the company's assets. Where there are assets to realise, the proceeds cover the cost of the work and the process may cost the directors nothing personally. Fees have to be approved by the creditors, the liquidation committee or the court, and the insolvency practitioner should set out the work they anticipate, what it is expected to cost, and the basis on which they are charging, before that basis is approved.

Where a company has no assets at all, someone has to fund the work, and that is a genuine problem faced by a lot of directors. There are still options, and the honest ones are worth hearing before you decide to simply stop trading and hope the company fades away. Walking away tends to create more personal risk than dealing with it does.

On timing, a straightforward company liquidation usually runs six to twelve months from appointment to closure, though the company stops trading almost immediately. The liquidation procedure itself is set by the Insolvency Act 1986, so the sequence does not vary much between cases; what varies is how long it takes to realise the assets and agree the claims.

One practical point that catches people out: any credit agreements, leases and hire purchase contracts in the company's name do not simply disappear. The liquidator deals with them, and where an agreement was personally guaranteed the lender will still look to the guarantor. Check what you have signed before you assume the company's closure ends the obligation. Cases involving property, disputed claims or an investigation take longer. An MVL for a solvent company can be quicker, particularly where the only real task is to distribute cash.

Who gets paid, and in what order

Once the company assets are realised, the proceeds are distributed in an order set by the Insolvency Act 1986. It is not first come first served, and it is not negotiable by the liquidator. Directors are often surprised by where they and their creditors actually sit.

  1. Secured creditors with a fixed chargeare paid from whatever their security is worth, outside the general pot
  2. The costs of the liquidationincluding the liquidator's fees, come next
  3. Preferential creditorswhere employees rank first for unpaid wages and holiday pay within statutory limits. These preferential payments come ahead of everything below them
  4. HMRCwhich since December 2020 ranks as a preferential creditor for VAT, and for the PAYE, employee National Insurance and CIS deductions it holds on the company's behalf. Corporation tax and employer National Insurance are not included and stay in the unsecured pot with everyone else
  5. The prescribed parta portion of whatever the floating charge assets bring in, which the law sets aside so that ordinary creditors are not left with nothing
  6. Floating charge holders
  7. Unsecured creditorswhich is most trade suppliers, and where any remaining HMRC debt sits
  8. Shareholdersonly if everything above has been paid in full, which in an insolvent liquidation does not happen

Two consequences matter in practice. HMRC's move up the order in 2020 pushed unsecured creditors further down, so trade suppliers recover less than they once did from the same estate. And a director who has lent money to the company is an unsecured creditor, near the back, which is why director loans are so rarely repaid in an insolvent winding-up.

It also changes how a rescue has to be built. Where HMRC is owed a significant amount of VAT or PAYE, it now holds a stronger position than it did before, and any proposal that depends on HMRC accepting a reduced return has to be realistic about that. If HMRC is your largest creditor, its view of the company assets and of what you are proposing tends to determine the outcome, whichever procedure you choose.

Partly preferential and partly unsecured, which catches people out. HMRC is a preferential creditor for VAT, and for the PAYE, employee National Insurance and CIS deductions the company collected on someone else's behalf. For corporation tax and employer National Insurance it ranks as an ordinary unsecured creditor alongside trade suppliers. So a company with a large VAT arrears position and a company with a large corporation tax bill face quite different distributions, even if the total owed to HMRC is the same.

What happens to employees

Employees are usually made redundant when the liquidator is appointed, because the company has stopped trading. They become creditors of the company for what they are owed, and they have preferential status for certain claims, which puts them ahead of ordinary unsecured creditors.

Where the company cannot pay, employees can apply to the Redundancy Payments Service for statutory redundancy pay, unpaid wages, notice pay and accrued holiday pay, which come out of the National Insurance Fund. Each element has its own statutory limit, there is a cap on a week's pay, and statutory redundancy pay requires two years' continuous service. Notice pay is treated as compensation, so it is reduced by anything earned or claimable elsewhere during the notice period. The liquidator provides the reference numbers and the information needed to claim, and dealing with this properly is usually one of the first things a good practitioner does.

If you are a director who is also an employee of the company, you may be able to claim from the Redundancy Payments Service too. It is frequently overlooked, and it is worth asking about.

After the liquidation ends

Once the liquidator has realised the assets, agreed the claims and distributed the proceeds, the company is dissolved and removed from the register at Companies House. It stops existing as a legal entity, and any remaining unpaid debts of the company die with it, apart from anything personally guaranteed.

For you as a director, three things follow. The conduct report goes to the Insolvency Service, and in the great majority of cases nothing further happens. Restrictions apply to reusing the company name, which are strict and carry personal liability if breached. And you are generally free to be a director of another limited company, unless you have been disqualified.

Many directors do go on to start again, and doing so lawfully is entirely possible. Doing it accidentally unlawfully, by reusing a prohibited name, is a trap worth avoiding with one conversation.

Liquidation in Scotland

The corporate insolvency framework in the Insolvency Act 1986 applies across Great Britain, but the process in Scotland differs in ways that matter. Court proceedings run through the Court of Session, or the local Sheriff Court where the company's paid-up share capital does not exceed £120,000, rather than the English courts, and Scottish procedure and terminology are not identical. For individuals, Scotland has its own regime entirely, with sequestration, protected trust deeds and the Debt Arrangement Scheme in place of English bankruptcy and IVAs.

Alternatives worth considering first

Liquidation is one outcome, not the only one. Depending on where the business actually stands:

Company voluntary arrangement
lets a viable company repay creditors over time and keep trading
Administration
creates breathing space and holds creditors back while a rescue or sale is arranged, and pre-pack administration allows an immediate sale of the business
Turnaround
addresses the cause rather than the symptom, where there is still a business worth saving
Debt restructuring
reshapes what the company owes without a formal insolvency procedure
Contingency planning
prepares a fallback before circumstances force the decision for you

gives you and any lender an objective read on whether the business is viable

The practical difference between the last two is control: in a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation you choose the timing and have a say in who is appointed, whereas Compulsory Liquidation is imposed on you.

A straightforward CVL usually takes six to twelve months to complete, though complex cases take longer. The company stops trading almost immediately, but the liquidator's work of realising assets, agreeing claims and reporting continues after that.
