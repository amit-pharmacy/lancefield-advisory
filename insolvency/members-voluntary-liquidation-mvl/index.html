Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL)

Usually shortened to MVL, a members' voluntary liquidation is how a limited company that can pay everything it owes is closed formally. The company directors declare that the company is solvent, the shareholders vote to wind it up, and a licensed insolvency practitioner is brought in as liquidator to settle any remaining liabilities and distribute the company's assets to the shareholders. The reason for doing it this way rather than a simple strike-off is tax: the money comes out as capital rather than income, which for most owners means a considerably smaller bill.

Is an MVL actually worth it for you?

We do not take insolvency appointments. Nobody here becomes your liquidator, so we earn nothing from recommending an MVL, and we will tell you when a strike-off does the job. That is the first question, so it comes first.

The figure everyone has heard is £25,000. It is real, but it is widely misunderstood. Under the Corporation Tax Act 2010, a company heading for strike-off can distribute up to £25,000 in total to its shareholders and have that treated as capital. Go a pound over and the whole distribution, not just the excess, is taxed as income. It is a cliff edge in the tax rules, not a break-even point for an MVL.

So the honest position is this:
Retained profits of £25,000 or less.
A strike-off almost always does the job. The distribution is capital anyway, and paying for a formal liquidation would be money wasted. One condition: the company has to be dissolved within two years of the distribution and its debts settled, or HMRC can withdraw the capital treatment and tax the whole amount as income
Retained profits comfortably above £25,000.
An MVL is usually worth it. The gap between capital gains tax and dividend tax on a six-figure distribution is normally many times the cost of the process
Somewhere near the line.
Do the arithmetic properly with your accountant before deciding. The practitioner's fee, your other gains in the year, and whether you qualify for Business Asset Disposal Relief all move the answer

If you have been told you need an MVL and nobody has shown you that arithmetic, ask for it. It takes ten minutes and it is the whole decision.

Who uses an MVL, and why

The same few situations account for nearly all of them.

Retirement.
The business has run its course, there is no buyer or no wish to sell, and the retained profits built up over the years need to come out
After a sale.
The trade and assets have been sold, the limited company is a shell holding the proceeds, and the shareholders want the money distributed
Contractors and consultants.
A personal service company is no longer needed, often because of a change in working arrangements or a move into employment, and it holds more cash than it is sensible to draw as dividends
Group tidy-ups.
Dormant or redundant subsidiaries cost money and carry risk for as long as they exist. Closing them through solvent liquidations is the core of corporate simplification
Part of a wider exit.
Where a founder is planning their way out of a business over a period of years, the MVL is often the final step in a longer piece of exit planning

What these have in common is that the company has done its job and its shareholders want a clean, final, defensible end to it. An MVL gives them that: a liquidator's final account, a dissolution at Companies House, and no loose ends for creditors or HMRC to pull on later.

The tax position, plainly

This is the reason most people choose a members' voluntary liquidation, so it is worth being exact about what it does and does not do. Your accountant will confirm your own numbers, and we work alongside them rather than instead of them.

Distributions paid out in a winding-up are treated as capital. You are taxed as if you had sold your shares for that amount, so capital gains tax applies rather than income tax on a dividend. For a higher-rate taxpayer that difference alone is substantial.

On top of that, many shareholders qualify for Business Asset Disposal Relief, which cuts the capital gains tax rate on the first £1 million of qualifying gains over a lifetime. The relief has become less generous than it was: the rate rose to 14% in April 2025 and to 18% from 6 April 2026. For a higher-rate shareholder it is still well below the rate on dividend income, though for a distribution small enough to stay inside the basic rate band it may not be, and without the relief the capital gains rate is 24%. Anyone working from the old figure of 10% is working from the wrong number. Qualifying depends on holding at least 5% of the ordinary shares and 5% of the votes, plus a 5% economic entitlement, which can be met either through profits and assets on a winding up or through the proceeds on a sale of the whole company, and on being an officer or employee throughout the two years before the company stops trading. The distributions also have to be made within three years of it ceasing to trade, which is a real trap where an MVL drags on, so check your own eligibility rather than assuming it.

There is one rule that catches people out, and it deserves its own paragraph.

The two-year rule

HMRC has a targeted anti-avoidance rule for exactly this situation. It applies where you held at least 5% of the company and the company was a close company, which covers almost every owner-managed business. If you take a capital distribution from a company in a winding-up and then, inside two years, you are back doing the same kind of work, or something close to it, through another company or as a sole trader, and saving tax was one of the main purposes of the arrangement, HMRC can tax those distributions as income after all. It is aimed at people who close a company, bank the money at capital rates, and start again on Monday.

If your plan genuinely is to retire, to take employment, or to do something different, the rule is not aimed at you. If your plan is to carry on the same business in a new company, say so before the MVL starts, because the tax saving you are counting on may not be there, and there may be a better way to do what you want.

How the MVL process runs

The procedure is set by the Insolvency Act 1986 and, for a Scottish company, the Scottish winding-up rules. The steps are the same in every case. What varies is how much has to be done before the first one.

  1. Get the company ready.Final accounts are prepared, the last corporation tax return is dealt with, and every creditor that can be paid is paid. Directors are usually advised to clear the company's debts before the winding-up starts rather than leaving them unpaid, because creditors are entitled to statutory interest on any debt still owed once it begins
  2. Statutory declaration of solvency.The directors, or a majority of them where there are more than two, make a formal declaration that they have made a full enquiry into the company's affairs and that it will meet every debt in full, with interest, within a period they specify of no more than twelve months from the start of the winding-up. It carries a statement of what the company owns and what it owes, is made before a notary public or a justice of the peace, or in England and Wales a solicitor, and must be made no more than five weeks before the shareholders' resolution. It is the document the whole process rests on
  3. Notice to any floating charge holder, then the resolution.Where the company has given a lender a floating charge that has never been discharged, written notice of the resolution goes to that lender first, and the resolution cannot be passed until five business days later unless the lender consents in writing. The shareholders then pass a special resolution to wind the company up and appoint the liquidator, either at a general meeting or by written resolution. A meeting needs at least fourteen days' notice unless members holding 90% of the voting rights agree to shorter notice; a written resolution avoids the notice period altogether and is how most MVLs are done. The winding-up resolution needs a 75% majority of those voting; the appointment itself needs only a simple majority. The declaration is delivered within 15 days of the resolution, to Companies House for a company registered in England and Wales and to the Accountant in Bankruptcy for one registered in Scotland, and notice of the resolution goes in the Gazette inside 14 days: the London Gazette for a company registered in England and Wales, the Edinburgh Gazette for one registered in Scotland
  4. The practitioner takes over.From appointment, the company's affairs and assets are in the liquidator's hands. The directors' powers cease, though in practice the directors are relied on for information and records
  5. Creditors are invited to claim.Notices invite claims and any liabilities still outstanding are settled. In a well-prepared MVL there is little or nothing left at this stage
  6. Distributions to shareholders.Distributions of cash are usually made in stages. A first payment is often made a few weeks after appointment, with the balance held back until it is clear nothing else is coming in. Distributions go to shareholders according to their rights, which means in proportion to their holdings where there is a single class of shares
  7. HMRC clearance.Confirmation is obtained from HMRC that there is no further tax to pay. This is the step that governs how long the whole thing takes
  8. Final account and dissolution.A final account of the winding-up is prepared and sent to the shareholders and to Companies House, and the dissolution of the company follows three months after it is registered

There is very little for you to do once the appointment is made, provided the preparation was done properly. The work is front-loaded, which is exactly why the preparation matters.

Distributing assets that are not cash

Asset distribution does not have to mean selling everything first. An asset can be handed over as it stands, which is called a distribution in specie, and it is useful more often than people expect. A property, a vehicle, an investment, or a debt owed to the company can all be transferred to a shareholder as part of their entitlement, asset by asset, which saves the cost and delay of a sale and keeps the asset where the owner wants it.

The most common use is a director's loan account. Where a director owes the company money, that debt can be distributed to the director, which cancels it against what the director was due to receive. That is lawful and routine. What it is not is a way of making an overdrawn loan account disappear for tax purposes, and it needs to be handled by the liquidator and your accountant together rather than assumed.

How long it takes, and what slows it down

A straightforward MVL, a company with cash at bank and nothing else, can see most of the money distributed within a few weeks of appointment. The formal end of the process, the final account and dissolution, usually takes longer, and six to twelve months from start to finish is a realistic expectation for most companies.

The things that stretch it are predictable:
Tax clearance.
The liquidator will not close the liquidation until HMRC confirms there is nothing further to pay, and HMRC's response times are outside anyone's control
Any asset other than cash.
Property has to be sold or transferred to a shareholder, debtors collected, investments realised
Unresolved liabilities.
A lease not yet surrendered, a contingent claim, a warranty given on the sale of the trade. Each is a debt or a risk that has to be dealt with or provided for before everything can be distributed
An overdrawn director's loan account
that nobody planned for
None of these stops an MVL. All of them are faster to resolve before the declaration of solvency is made than afterwards.

What it costs

The liquidator's fee is agreed at the outset and paid from the company's assets, together with the disbursements of the process: the statutory advertisements, the bond, or caution in Scotland (every appointed insolvency practitioner must be covered by a surety or cautioner against loss caused by their own fraud or dishonesty), and similar costs. For a straightforward solvent company most practitioners quote a fixed fee plus disbursements, and you should expect to be told the figure before you commit to anything.

We do not set that fee and we will not quote a number here that turns out to be wrong for your company. What we will do is tell you what the practitioner we introduce charges before you meet them and, more usefully, show you whether the tax saving clears it by a wide enough margin to make the whole exercise worthwhile.

What if the company is not as solvent as you think?

This is the risk in an MVL, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because the process feels benign.

The declaration of solvency is a statutory declaration. Making it without reasonable grounds for believing the company can meet its debts in full within the stated period is a criminal offence. And if the debts are not in fact paid or provided for within that period, the law presumes the directors did not have reasonable grounds unless they can show otherwise. So "I thought it was fine" is not a comfortable place to be standing.

If the liquidator forms the view that the company will not be able to pay its debts in full within the stated period, the process does not stop. It converts into a creditors' voluntary liquidation, the creditors come in, and the directors' conduct is reported on, as it is in any insolvent winding-up. What began as a tidy closure becomes something else entirely.

The practical protection is simple: before anyone signs anything, be certain. HMRC is always one of the creditors until it confirms otherwise. Corporation tax on the final period, VAT on the final return, PAYE, a director's loan account that has been running the wrong way, a lease, a guarantee given to a customer, a dispute that might turn into a claim. Each of those is a debt until it is settled. If there is any doubt about whether the company is solvent, that is a different conversation, and our liquidation page sets out how the solvent and insolvent routes part company.

MVL compared with the alternatives

MVL or strike-off

Both end the company. Strike-off is cheaper and simpler, and for small retained profits it is the right answer. Its limits are the £25,000 cap on capital treatment, the fact that any creditor can object, which in practice suspends the striking-off while Companies House considers it, and a strike-off that stalls past two years costs you the capital treatment, and the lack of any formal line drawn under the company's affairs. An MVL costs more and takes longer, and in return gives capital treatment on all distributions, a formal final account, and a clean dissolution.

MVL or CVL

The difference between an MVL and a creditors' voluntary liquidation is solvency. A CVL is for an insolvent company; an MVL is for one that can pay every debt. They are not alternatives you choose between. The company's balance sheet chooses for you, and getting that wrong in either direction is expensive.

MVL or carrying on

Some shareholders keep a company open purely to draw the retained profits as dividends over several years, staying under the higher thresholds. That is legitimate, but it costs accountancy fees and filing obligations for every year it runs, and it only works if you do not need the money sooner. Compare the two on your own numbers rather than by instinct.

MVL or administration

Rarely confused, but worth stating: administration is a protective procedure for a company in difficulty. If the business is solvent and simply finished, administration has nothing to offer and an MVL is the right route.

MVLs in Scotland

The Insolvency Act 1986 applies across Great Britain, so the declaration of solvency, the 12-month test and the shareholders' resolution are the same for a Scottish company as for an English one. The procedural rules differ. A company registered in Scotland follows the Scottish winding-up rules rather than the England and Wales rules, the notices go in the Edinburgh Gazette, and some filings go somewhere different: the declaration of solvency and the liquidator's notice of appointment are delivered to the Accountant in Bankruptcy rather than Companies House, and the final account goes to both.

None of that changes the decision, but it does mean the practitioner you use should handle Scottish liquidations as a matter of routine rather than occasionally. Ours do; Glasgow is where we work.
