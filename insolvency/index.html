Insolvency

What an insolvency practitioner does, and how to choose one

Certain roles in an insolvency can only be held by someone the law has licensed to hold them. Liquidator, administrator, nominee and supervisor of a company voluntary arrangement, and in Scotland trustee in a sequestration or under a protected trust deed: each of those is a formal appointment, and each is closed to anyone without a licence. Taking one without being qualified is a criminal offence under section 389 of the Insolvency Act 1986.

What they actually do

An insolvency practitioner's job changes depending on the appointment. Across all of them, they take control of a situation that has become too serious to resolve informally, and they owe duties to creditors as a whole rather than to whoever hired them.

Liquidator
in a creditors' voluntary liquidation, members' voluntary liquidation or compulsory liquidation, realising assets and distributing proceeds to creditors. The general shape of the process is covered on our liquidation page
Administrator
taking control of a company to rescue it, sell it, or achieve a better outcome for creditors than an immediate liquidation. Where a sale is arranged in advance, that is a pre-pack administration
Nominee and then supervisor
of a company voluntary arrangement, putting the proposal to creditors and then policing it
Trustee
in a sequestration or supervisor of a trust deed, on the personal side. Elsewhere in the UK the equivalent personal procedures are bankruptcy and Individual Voluntary Arrangements
Receiver
under a fixed charge over property, which in a property context is an LPA receivership
Investigating director conduct
and reporting on it, which is a statutory duty in every insolvent appointment

They also produce a Statement of Affairs, deal with employees and the Redundancy Payments Service, advertise appointments in the Gazette, and file at Companies House. In compulsory company liquidations the Official Receiver acts first, and may then hand over to a licensed practitioner.

How they are licensed and regulated

This matters more than most people realise, because it is the thing you can actually check.

A licence means sitting the exams set by the Joint Insolvency Examinations Board, the JIEB, and then securing authorisation from a recognised professional body. In Scotland that is usually ICAS, the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Scotland. Elsewhere in the UK it may be ICAEW, the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales, or the Insolvency Practitioners Association. They must also hold a bond, and follow the Insolvency Code of Ethics and the Statements of Insolvency Practice, which set out how particular procedures must be conducted.

Insolvency law in the UK sits mainly in the Insolvency Act 1986, with separate Scottish rules for court procedure and a separate statute again for personal insolvency in Scotland. An insolvency practitioner has to work within all of it, and the licence is the state's way of confirming they can.

The Insolvency Service maintains a public register on its website, and it takes a minute to check. As the regulator of last resort it oversees the recognised professional bodies, each of which acts as the direct regulator for its own licence holders, and complaints about any practitioner are routed through a single complaints gateway. Professionals who fall short can lose their licence.

For guidance on choosing and on what to expect, R3, the trade body for the profession, publishes free material, as does the Insolvency Service. Neither will give you advice about your own company, but both are a reasonable place to start reading.

The part nobody in this industry likes to say out loud

An insolvency practitioner is paid from the appointment. The fee comes out of the estate, and the appointment only exists if a formal procedure happens.

That does not make practitioners dishonest. Most are careful, regulated professionals doing a difficult job well. But it does mean that when you ask someone whose income depends on formal appointments whether you need a formal appointment, you are asking a question they have an interest in the answer to.

Lancefield does not take appointments. Nobody here becomes your liquidator, administrator or trustee, so we earn nothing from recommending one. That is deliberate, and it is the reason clients and professional contacts come to us first. Where a formal appointment genuinely is needed, we bring in a licensed practitioner we know and trust, and we stay alongside you through it.

What it costs

Fees depend on the procedure and the complexity of the estate. In most insolvent appointments the practitioner is paid from company assets rather than by the directors personally, so where a company has assets to realise, the process may cost you nothing directly.

Where a company has no assets, someone has to fund the work, and directors are often asked to. Fees must be approved by creditors or the court, and practitioners are required to provide a fees estimate and explain the basis of their remuneration before drawing anything. If you are given a number without that explanation, ask for it.

Do you need one yet?

Often, not yet. An insolvency practitioner is required for a formal procedure. A great many situations are resolved before that point, and the earlier you look at it, the more of those options remain open.

The distinction that matters is between financial distress and formal insolvency. Distress is a cash flow problem: late payments, stretched credit, a lender getting nervous. Insolvency is a legal state, either being unable to pay debts as they fall due or having liabilities that exceed assets. Plenty of businesses in real distress are not yet insolvent, and for those the answer is finance, restructuring or a hard look at the numbers, not an appointment. Bringing in an insolvency practitioner at that stage can foreclose options that were still available.

You probably need advice, not an appointment

If creditor pressure is building but nothing formal has started, if you are weighing whether the business is viable, or if you want to understand your exposure as a director, that is advisory work. It does not require an appointment. Support at this stage might mean turnaround work, debt restructuring, an independent business review to establish where the business actually stands, or simply getting the legal position clear. Acting before you are forced to is what contingency planning is for.

You probably do need a practitioner

If a winding up petition has been advertised, if you have decided to close an insolvent company, or if you want to place the business into administration, a licensed insolvency practitioner has to be appointed to carry it out. At that point the insolvency process is under way and the question is who runs it, not whether.

One further benefit of asking early is that it involves the other stakeholders on your terms rather than theirs. Lenders, landlords and HMRC all respond better to a director who approaches them with a plan than to one who has stopped answering the phone, and finance options that exist in month one are frequently gone by month four. Debts do not improve with silence.

letting a viable company repay creditors over time and keep trading. This is the main rescue route where the business itself still works

If a creditor has already moved against you, start with winding up petition, because that is the situation with the shortest fuse.

Choosing well, in Scotland

Scotland has its own insolvency regime for individuals and its own court procedure for companies. Sequestration, protected trust deeds and the Debt Arrangement Scheme have no direct English equivalents, and Scottish corporate matters run through the Court of Session or the Sheriff Court rather than the English courts. A national firm working from English precedent will get some of this wrong.

When you are choosing, ask three things. Is the person who will actually handle your case licensed, and with which body. What are the realistic alternatives to the procedure being recommended. And what happens to your personal position, including any personal guarantees, under each option. A good practitioner will answer all three without hesitating.

That third question is the one directors most often leave until it is too late. If you have signed a personal guarantee, the company procedure and your own finances are two halves of the same problem, and they need to be looked at together. Our Debt Help Scotland section covers the personal side.
