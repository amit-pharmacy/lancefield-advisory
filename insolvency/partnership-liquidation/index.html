Dissolving a partnership

A traditional partnership has no separate legal shell to hide behind in England and Wales, and even in Scotland, where it does have its own legal personality, the partners stand behind its debts. That single fact shapes everything on this page. Dissolving a partnership is not just an administrative exercise in closing a business: it decides who pays what, and if the firm cannot pay, the answer is its members, personally, from their own assets.

The question to settle first: can the partnership pay its debts?

Everything else follows from this, and partners routinely reach the end of a dissolution before facing it.

If the partnership is solvent

This is a winding up of the business affairs: realise the assets, pay the creditors in full, settle the accounts between the partners, and distribute what is left. It can still be difficult, particularly where partners disagree about value, but nobody's house is at risk and the process is essentially one of accounting and agreement.

If it is not

Then the partnership debts are the partners' debts. Creditors can pursue any one of them for the whole of what is owed, not merely a share, and go after personal assets to get it. At that point the question stops being how to dissolve the partnership and becomes how each partner deals with a personal debt problem, which is where sequestration, trust deeds and the rest of our Debt Help Scotland section come in.

We are advisers on the second of these, and we work alongside your solicitor on the first. Partners in a struggling firm often spend months negotiating the split while the debt grows, when the useful conversation is about the debt.

What dissolution actually means

Legal advice on partnership dissolution distinguishes two things, and the distinction matters in practice.

A technical dissolution happens when the partnership as a legal arrangement comes to an end but the business carries on, usually with the remaining partners under a new partnership. A partner taking their exit from a firm that continues without them is the common example.

A general dissolution is the end of the business partnership itself: the firm is dissolved, the assets are sold, the creditors are paid and the affairs are wound up. That is what most people mean by dissolving a partnership, and it is what this page is mainly about.

In either case the partnership continues to exist for the limited purpose of winding up. The partners keep the authority they need to complete unfinished business and to realise the assets, and they remain bound by their duties to each other while they do it. What they cannot do is take on new obligations that have nothing to do with the wind-up.

How a partnership is dissolved

If there is a partnership agreement, it governs. A well-drafted agreement will say how a partner can retire, what notice is required, how the assets are valued, what happens on death or bankruptcy, and how disputes are resolved. Follow it: the courts will, and departing from it is how partnership disputes start.

Without an agreement, the Partnership Act 1890 supplies the default rules, and they surprise people. Under the Act, and subject to anything the partners have agreed:

  1. Any partner can dissolve a partnership at will by serving notice to dissolveon the others. No reason is needed, no notice period applies unless the agreement provides one, and it takes effect from the date the notice to dissolve states
  2. The death or bankruptcy of any partner dissolves the whole firmunless the partners have agreed otherwise. This is the rule that catches families and long-standing firms, and it is the single strongest argument for having an agreement
  3. A fixed-term partnership dissolves when the term expiresor the venture it was formed for is completed
  4. The court can make an order to dissolve the firmon the application of a partner, where a partner is permanently incapable, has behaved in a way that makes it impracticable to carry on with them, has breached the agreement, or where the firm can no longer trade at anything but a loss
  5. The partnership ends automatically if the business becomes unlawful

Partners should also give notice of the partnership dissolution to anyone who had business relationships with the firm, and publish it in the Gazette. Until third parties know, a former partner can still be held liable for obligations the continuing partners take on, because the outside world is entitled to rely on the position it knew about.

Winding up the business: the steps

  1. Read the partnership agreement, or establish that there is not one.Everything that follows depends on it, and this is the point at which legal advice is cheapest
  2. Agree the date of dissolution and record it in writingalong with who is doing what during the wind-up
  3. Notify.Customers, suppliers, the landlord, the bank, insurers, HMRC: every business relationship the firm has. Publish in the Gazette, and cancel or reassign contracts, licences and direct debits
  4. Deal with employees.Dissolution usually ends the employment relationship; where the business is sold or continues under a new partnership, TUPE may apply and the staff transfer
  5. Realise the assets.Sell the stock, plant and property, collect the debtors, and decide what happens to the goodwill, the intellectual property and the business name, which is often the most contested item and rarely the most valuable
  6. Pay in the statutory order.Under the Act, the assets go first to outside creditors, then to repay partners' advances beyond their capital, then to repay capital, and only then is any surplus divided between the partners in the profit-sharing ratio
  7. Settle the accounts between partners.Final accounts are drawn up and the balances between partners are worked out. Where the assets do not cover the liabilities and capital, the deficiency is borne by the partners in the same proportion as they shared profits
  8. Deal with tax.The partnership makes a final return, each partner reports their share, and there may be capital gains or balancing charges on the assets. Your accountant should be in this from the start

The step partners skip is the notification. It is dull, it is cheap, and leaving it undone is how a departed partner discovers they are still liable for something a year later.

Who is liable for the debts

This is the part that changes lives, so it is worth being exact about it.

Every partner is liable, along with the others, for the liabilities the firm takes on while they are a partner, and in England and Wales that liability is joint, with a partner's estate also severally liable after death. In Scotland the partners are jointly and severally liable for the firm's debts. In plain terms: a creditor can pursue whichever partner is most able to pay for the whole amount, and it is then for that partner to seek a contribution from the others, which is only worth anything if the others have assets.

A few consequences follow that partners often get wrong:
Retiring does not end liability for what already exists.
A partner who exits stays liable for the liabilities the firm took on during their time in it, unless the creditor agrees to release them
Nor does an indemnity from the continuing partners.
An agreement between partners binds the partners, not the creditor. The creditor can still come to you; the indemnity is only as good as the person who gave it
Joining a firm does not import its history.
Liabilities the firm ran up before someone became a partner are not theirs, unless they expressly agree to shoulder them
Failing to notify third parties leaves you exposed.
Without notice of your retirement or the dissolution, someone who dealt with the firm can still treat you as a partner
The debts are personal.
There is no limited liability to fall back on, which is why a partnership that cannot pay is a personal insolvency problem for each partner

If you are approaching that point, the advice is the same as for a director facing personal exposure: get the whole picture in front of one adviser, early, before options start closing. Our Debt Help Scotland section covers the personal routes, and where a viable business is buried under historic debt, debt restructuring or a turnaround review may reach a better answer than dissolution.

When the partners cannot agree

Most partnership disputes are about three things: whether the firm should end at all, what the assets are worth, and who has taken more than their share. The dissolution itself is often the easy part.

Where negotiation fails, a partner can apply to the court, and court orders can both dissolve the firm and appoint a receiver or a manager to run the business and wind up its affairs. That is expensive, slow, public, and consumes the value everyone is arguing about. Mediation, or an agreed independent valuation, resolves most of these disputes for a fraction of the cost, and it is worth exhausting those before anyone issues proceedings.

The other route worth naming: rather than dissolving, the partners can agree that one buys the others out and the business partnership carries on with a different line-up. Where there is a viable business and the argument is about people, that is frequently the better outcome for everyone, and it is a conversation an adviser without a stake in the fight can often start.

Partnerships in Scotland are different

In Scotland a partnership is a legal person distinct from its partners. It can own property and intellectual property, contract, sue and be sued in the firm's own name, and it can be sequestrated as an entity separately from the partners' own estates. The partners remain jointly and severally liable for the firm's debts, so separate personality is not limited liability, but it does change the mechanics.

In practice that means an insolvent Scottish partnership has options that read differently from the guidance written for England and Wales. The firm can be sequestrated; the partners can be sequestrated; a trust deed may be the better route for an individual partner; and the interaction between the firm's estate and each partner's estate needs to be thought about deliberately rather than assumed. Most of the material online about dissolving a partnership does not address any of this, because it is written about English law. Scottish firms and Scottish partners are what we deal with from Glasgow.
