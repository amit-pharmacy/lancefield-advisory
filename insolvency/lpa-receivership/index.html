LPA receivership

An LPA receiver is someone a lender appoints to take control of a property it holds a fixed charge over, usually to collect the rental income and, in most cases, to sell it. The power comes from the Law of Property Act 1925, which is where the name comes from: this has nothing to do with a lasting power of attorney, which is a different thing with the same initials. You will also see the role called a fixed charge receiver. Fixed charge receivership and LPA receivership are the same thing.

Two things set it apart from everything else in this section. It is not an insolvency procedure: no court is involved, nobody is made bankrupt or wound up, and the rest of the business is untouched. And it is an England and Wales remedy. Property in Scotland is enforced under a different statute, with different steps, covered below.

If a receiver has just been appointed over your property

This is the version most people arrive here with, so it comes first.

From the day of appointment the receiver controls the asset, and everything they do is aimed at clearing the secured debt from it. Tenants are told to pay their rent to the receiver, who manages and insures the building and, in nearly every modern case, can sell it too, because the loan documents extend the statutory powers that far. You still own the property until it is sold and, as borrower, you still owe the debt. The costs of the receivership process are added to what you owe, and if the eventual sale does not clear the secured loan, the shortfall remains yours.

The part that surprises people is that the receiver is, in law, your agent. The 1925 Act makes the receiver the borrower's agent rather than the lender's, so you carry responsibility for what the receiver does while the lender is largely insulated from it. The receiver's duties are to act in good faith, to get a proper price on any sale, and to account for the money. They are not duties to look after your interests.

What you can still do:
Pay the debt, or refinance it.
The receivership ends when the lender is paid. If there is equity, bridging finance or a new commercial mortgage can sometimes redeem the existing lender before a forced disposal crystallises the loss. Speed matters, because a receiver rarely achieves the price you would
Negotiate.
Lenders appoint receivers to get repaid, not because they want to own buildings. A credible proposal to clear arrears or sell in an orderly way is sometimes accepted even after appointment
Check the appointment is valid.
The power depends on the charge being by deed, the money being due, and the conditions for enforcement having been met. For a company, check whether the fixed charge was registered at Companies House. Non-registration will not by itself unwind the appointment, because an unregistered charge is void only against a liquidator, an administrator and creditors, but it changes everything if the company then goes into a formal procedure. Defective appointments happen, and solicitors can test one quickly
Look at the whole position.
A receiver over one building is often the first visible step in a wider problem. If the company that owns it cannot pay its other creditors, the real question for the creditors as a whole is no longer the receiver; it is whether the company needs administration, a company voluntary arrangement or liquidation, and those decisions are better made now than after the disposal
What you cannot do is ignore it. The receiver does not need your cooperation.

When can a lender appoint an LPA receiver?

The power arises where the security is a mortgage or charge made by deed and the secured money has become due. The Act then restricts when it can be used: in outline, after a notice requiring payment has gone unanswered for three months, where interest is two months or more in arrears, or where some other term of the charge has been breached. In practice almost every commercial facility widens these rights so that a receiver can be appointed on any default, and it is the deed rather than the Act that governs the timing.

The appointment itself is simple. The bank signs a deed of appointment, the receiver accepts, and the receivership process has begun. No court approval is needed and, for an individual owner, nothing is filed anywhere. Where the owner is a company or an LLP, the lender must notify Companies House within seven days, and the appointment then appears on the public record.

The receiver can be anyone the lender chooses, provided they are not a body corporate. Where the borrower is a company there is a further bar, because it is an offence for an undischarged bankrupt, or someone subject to a bankruptcy or debt relief restrictions order, to act. Where the borrower is an individual, neither restriction applies. They do not need to hold an insolvency licence, and many are chartered surveyors working through specialist LPA receivership practices, which is why the property profession runs its own registration schemes for receivers. The bank picks; the owner has no say.

What an LPA receiver actually does

The statutory powers are narrow: to demand and recover the income from the property, to give receipts, to insure it if the lender directs that in writing, and to apply the money in a fixed order. Collecting rent and chasing rent debtors is the core of it. Almost everything else, including selling, comes from the deed, though the lender can also delegate its own statutory leasing powers to the receiver, which is why the first thing a competent receiver does is read it. In a typical receivership process the receiver will:

  • Notify tenants, collect the rental income, pursue debtors and arrears, and manage the asset day to day
  • Put insurance in place from day one, since a lapse in cover is the quickest way to lose value
  • Instruct professionals, usually solicitors and valuers, on title, value and marketing
  • Decide whether to hold and let or to sell
  • Market and sell the property, taking proper care over the price, which the courts measure against what the asset would fetch on the open market when it is sold
  • Apply the proceeds: the costs of the receivership, then the lender's debt, then any surplus to the owner or to the next secured creditor

The receiver's remuneration is a commission on the money recovered, capped by the Act at five per cent of the gross sum unless the deed or a court order allows more. Under the Act that five per cent also has to cover the receiver's own costs and expenses; it is the deed that puts the professionals' fees on top, and those professionals answer to the receiver, not to you. It all comes out of the asset before the owner sees anything.

LPA receiver, administrator, liquidator: the difference

LPA receiver

Appointed by a lender over a specific charged property. Deals with that asset only. No court, no moratorium, not an insolvency procedure. The owner keeps running the rest of the business.

Administrator

Appointed over the whole company by the directors, the company, a floating charge holder or the court. A licensed insolvency practitioner. Brings a moratorium that stops creditors, including most fixed charge holders, enforcing without consent. Our administration page explains it.

Liquidator

Appointed to wind the company up and distribute what is left to creditors. A licensed insolvency practitioner. A secured creditor stands outside the winding up and can still enforce, which is why a receiver can be appointed before, during or after it.

The thing to take from the comparison is scope. A receiver is one lender's debt recovery tool over one asset. The other two are procedures for the company as a whole. If your problem is one building, receivership is what you are facing. If the building is one symptom of a company that cannot pay its creditors, you are facing something larger, and the receiver is a detail.

How receivership interacts with insolvency

The question directors ask most is whether putting the company into a formal procedure stops the receiver. It depends which.

Administration.
A creditor needs the administrator's consent or court approval to enforce security once a company is in that procedure, and the administrator can require a receiver already in office to step aside. That can interrupt a receivership, but only where the procedure is genuine, and a bank holding a qualifying floating charge can appoint an administrator of its own choosing
Winding up.
Does not stop a secured creditor. The receiver's agency for the company ends when a liquidator is appointed, but the receiver can carry on, and the lender keeps its security
A CVA.
Cannot bind a secured creditor without consent, so it does not stop a receiver either
Personal insolvency.
Bankruptcy or, in Scotland, sequestration leaves the secured lender's rights over the property intact

None of these makes a receivership go away. They are decisions about the company or the individual, to be taken on their own merits.

How a receivership ends

When the lender is repaid and the receiver is discharged, when the asset is sold and the proceeds distributed, or when the lender removes the receiver. A surplus after the secured debt and costs belongs to the owner or the next secured creditor. A shortfall is still owed, and for a company director that is the point at which a personal guarantee, if one was given, becomes the live issue. That crossover, from a property problem to a personal one, is where most of the real damage happens, and it is why the decisions in the weeks before a receiver is appointed matter more than the ones after.

We advise lenders and other fixed charge holders as well as owners. For a lender the questions are always the same: is the charge valid and registered, has the money fallen due, have the deed's conditions been met, and is this the right receiver for this asset. The appointment is fast and cheap compared with a court process, which is its attraction, but a defective one costs more to unwind than it would have cost to check, and where the borrower's real problem is a failing business, a receiver may recover the debt while leaving a relationship to manage.

Property in Scotland: there are no LPA receivers

The Law of Property Act 1925 applies in England and Wales only. A lender holding a standard security over property in Scotland enforces it under the Conveyancing and Feudal Reform (Scotland) Act 1970, and the steps are different. The lender serves a calling-up notice, which demands payment, or a notice of default, which calls on the borrower to put a breach right. Once that notice period has run without the demand being met, the standard conditions give the lender its remedies: selling, entering into possession and drawing the rents, letting, and carrying out repairs. Where the property is used to any extent for residential purposes, and a flat above a shop counts, the lender cannot sell or take possession without either a warrant from the sheriff, which it can only apply for once it has met the pre-action requirements and shown it made real efforts to agree something else, or, where the property is empty, the written consent of everyone with an interest in it.

Company borrowers in Scotland are different again. Receivership in Scotland means a receiver appointed under a floating charge, a route the Enterprise Act largely closed for floating charges created on or after 15 September 2003, so for most Scottish companies the lender's real options are enforcing its standard security or appointing an administrator. If your property is in Scotland and a lender is threatening action, the LPA guidance you find online is describing another country's law.
