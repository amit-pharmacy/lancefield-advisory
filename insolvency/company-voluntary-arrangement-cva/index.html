Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA)

A company voluntary arrangement is a legally binding deal between an insolvent company and its creditors. The company keeps trading, and instead of paying its historic debt in full, it pays an affordable amount over an agreed period, usually from future profits. Whatever remains unpaid at the end is written off.

CVAs are the main rescue procedure in which the directors stay in control of a limited company that is insolvent but still has a viable business underneath the debt. Directors stay in control throughout, which is the feature that distinguishes CVAs from almost every other formal insolvency procedure. Retailers and hospitality groups have used CVAs to renegotiate rents across a portfolio, but they are just as available to a single owner-managed company.

How a CVA works

The mechanics matter, because the whole thing turns on one vote.

  • The directors work with a licensed insolvency practitioner, acting as nominee, to build a proposal setting out what the company can realistically afford to pay and over what period
  • The nominee reviews it and reports to the court on whether it is viable and worth putting to creditors
  • Creditors vote through a decision procedure, usually by correspondence or electronic voting rather than at a meeting. The proposal is approved if 75% or more by value of those who respond vote in favour, and exactly 75% is enough. A physical meeting is only held if creditors requisition one
  • Clearing 75% is not the whole test. A second count is taken of the unconnected creditors only, and if creditors holding more than half of the total admitted unconnected value vote it down, the proposal falls anyway. That count is against all admitted unconnected claims, not only the ones that voted. Unconnected means creditors who are not connected with the company, so money owed to directors, their families or associated companies is stripped out. The purpose is to stop a company approving its own rescue on the strength of debt it owes to itself, and in a small company carrying large director loans this is frequently the test that decides the outcome
  • Voting is on unsecured debt. The claim of a fully secured creditor counts as nil, and a partly secured claim counts only for the unsecured part
  • Approval binds every creditor who was entitled to vote, or who would have been entitled had they been given notice, not merely the ones who backed it. A creditor who opposed the proposal, or who ignored the vote entirely, is caught by it just the same. Secured and preferential creditors are bound only on terms that leave their security and their priority intact, unless they agree otherwise
  • The practitioner becomes supervisor, monitoring the payments and reporting to creditors
  • The company trades on, pays as agreed, and the balance of the debt is written off at the end

A CVA typically runs for three to five years. Proposals normally provide that interest and charges on the arrangement debts stop, and a creditor bound by the arrangement has no claim to pursue for the compromised debt while the company complies with it, so legal action to recover that debt cannot continue. There is no automatic court moratorium: the protection comes from the terms of the arrangement itself.

Who has to agree, and why HMRC usually decides

In most small and mid-sized company CVAs, HMRC is the largest single creditor, because the debt has built up as unpaid VAT and PAYE. That means HMRC's vote frequently determines the outcome.

There is a further point that changes what a proposal can offer. HMRC has held secondary preferential status since 1 December 2020 for the taxes a company collects on someone else's behalf: VAT, PAYE, employee National Insurance and CIS deductions. Those debts cannot be written down or ranked behind ordinary unsecured creditors without HMRC's agreement, so in a company whose arrears are mostly VAT and PAYE the arrangement is usually structured to pay that element in full over time. Corporation tax and employer's National Insurance remain ordinary unsecured debts and can be compromised in the usual way.

HMRC has a published approach to CVAs and will engage with proposals that are realistic, properly evidenced and honest about what went wrong. It reacts badly to optimistic forecasting and to proposals from directors who have ignored correspondence for a year. This is one of the clearest reasons why acting earlier produces better outcomes than acting later.

A secured creditor sits outside the arrangement unless it agrees to be bound, so a bank with a fixed charge keeps its security. Employees have preferential status for certain claims.

When a CVA is the right answer

It suits you if

The business is profitable, or would be without the historic debt. There is enough cash flow to fund meaningful contributions. The cause of the problem has been identified and fixed, whether that was a bad contract, a failed expansion or a single large loss. And you want to preserve the company, its contracts, its licences and its people.

It does not suit you if

The business loses money at an operating level. The forecasts only work if trading improves substantially. The debt is so large that any affordable contribution would look derisory to creditors. Or you would rather stop than commit to another three to five years.

We will tell you honestly which of these describes your company. If a CVA would only delay a liquidation by two years and cost you more in the meantime, that is worth knowing before you start rather than after.

The honest disadvantages

Pages selling CVAs tend to be light on this section. It deserves proper treatment, because CVAs are demanding and not every company should attempt one.

  • CVAs are a formal insolvency procedure, publicly recorded, and they affect the company's credit rating and its standing with suppliers and other stakeholders
  • Some suppliers will move to pro forma terms, and some contracts contain clauses triggered by an insolvency event
  • It is demanding. Three to five years of committed payments while running a business is not a soft option
  • If the company defaults, the supervisor can petition to wind it up, and you may end up in liquidation having spent the intervening period paying
  • Not all CVAs succeed. A meaningful proportion fail, usually because the original forecasts were too optimistic
  • Personal guarantees are unaffected. A guarantee you have signed remains enforceable against you personally

CVA compared with the alternatives

CVA or administration

Administration gives immediate protection from creditors through a moratorium and puts an administrator in control. It suits a business that needs breathing space right now, or one being prepared for sale, which is where a pre-pack administration comes in. A company voluntary arrangement gives no automatic moratorium in the same way, but directors stay in charge. Sometimes the two insolvency procedures combine, with administration used to stabilise and a CVA to exit.

CVA or liquidation

Liquidation closes the company. A CVA keeps it alive. The test is simply whether there is a viable business worth keeping. If there is not, a CVA delays the inevitable at cost, and a CVL deals with it honestly instead.

CVA or informal arrangement

If only one or two creditors are the problem, a negotiated arrangement or debt restructuring may achieve the same thing without any formal insolvency procedure at all. Where the debt is spread across many unsecured creditors, the binding effect of a CVA is exactly what makes it work. A turnaround review will usually tell you which of the two you are actually looking at.

CVA or winding up petition

If a creditor has already presented a winding up petition, the timetable changes completely. A CVA proposal takes weeks to build and the petition may not wait. It can still be the right answer, but it has to start immediately rather than after the hearing.

What a CVA does not do

Three limits are worth stating plainly, because proposals sometimes get built on assumptions that do not hold.

It does not override security or priority. A bank with a fixed charge, or any other secured creditor, keeps its right to enforce that security unless it agrees otherwise, and a preferential creditor keeps its priority. Only the ordinary unsecured debt can be compromised. That distinction shapes the whole proposal: if most of the debt sits with a secured creditor, a CVA may not reach the part of the problem that actually matters, and a different insolvency procedure will serve you better.

It does not remove personal guarantees. If you have guaranteed company borrowing, the guarantee survives the arrangement, and the lender can still pursue you personally even while the company keeps paying. Directors are regularly caught by this, and where it applies the personal position needs its own answer alongside the company one.

It does not fix a business that does not work. A company voluntary arrangement reschedules debt; it does not create cash flow. If the underlying trading position is loss-making, the arrangement will fail at the first missed payment, and the company will be in a worse position than if it had dealt with matters directly.

How the payments actually work

Most CVAs run on monthly payments out of trading profit, set at a level the business can genuinely sustain rather than the maximum it could manage in a good month. The figure is built from a cash flow forecast, and the forecast is the part creditors scrutinise hardest, because an over-optimistic one is the most common reason arrangements fail. A forecast is only as reliable as the financial reporting systems behind it, so getting monthly management figures in order is part of preparing a credible proposal.

Some proposals also bring in a lump sum, from selling assets the business does not need, or from a third party such as a director or an investor. Where a company has assets that are not central to trading, realising them early tends to make the proposal considerably more attractive to unsecured creditors than a longer schedule of monthly payments would.

If circumstances change, the supervisor can put a variation to creditors. That is worth knowing, because directors often assume the terms are fixed for the full term and stop communicating when trading dips. Missed payments without explanation are what lead to a failed arrangement; a variation proposed early usually does not.

What it costs

There are two elements: the nominee's fee for preparing and reporting on the proposal, and the supervisor's fees for running the arrangement, which are normally drawn from the contributions rather than paid separately. Fees have to be disclosed and approved by creditors, so the arrangement should be transparent about what the practitioner takes.

The relevant comparison is not the fee in isolation. It is what creditors would receive in a liquidation instead, which is usually very little, against what they receive under the arrangement. A proposal that beats the liquidation comparison is the one that passes.
