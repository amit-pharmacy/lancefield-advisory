Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL)

When an insolvent company is closed by its own directors instead of by a creditor going to court, the procedure used is a creditors' voluntary liquidation. The decision is yours, so is most of the timing, and you have a say in which insolvency practitioner is appointed. Directors rarely appreciate how much that matters until they have lost the option.

It is the most common formal insolvency procedure in the UK, and for a company that genuinely cannot pay its debts it is usually the right answer. What it is not is a way of making the problem disappear discreetly. This is a formal process with real consequences for you as a director, and those consequences are worth understanding before you commit rather than after.

Is a CVL actually the right answer for you?

We do not take insolvency appointments. Nobody here becomes your liquidator, so we earn nothing from telling you to liquidate. That is why this section comes first rather than last.

A creditors' voluntary liquidation is the right answer where the company is insolvent, there is no realistic prospect of trading out of it, and continuing would simply increase the debt. That describes a great many companies, and if it describes yours then the sooner it is dealt with the better your own position will be.

It is the wrong answer, or at least premature, in three situations that come up repeatedly.
The business underneath is viable and the debt is historic.
A company voluntary arrangement can bind the whole creditor body and let a working business trade on. Liquidation ends it permanently
One creditor is the problem, not the whole book.
Negotiation or debt restructuring may deal with it without any formal insolvency procedure at all
The company can pay its debts and you just want it closed.
That is an MVL, a completely different procedure with a very different tax outcome. Putting a solvent company through a CVL would be an expensive mistake

Ask any adviser who recommends liquidation what the alternatives are and why they were ruled out. If the answer is thin, get a second view. A turnaround review or an independent review will tell you whether there is anything worth saving, and both are cheaper than finding out afterwards.

How the process runs

The liquidation process is set by the Insolvency Act 1986 and does not vary much between cases. What varies is how long each stage takes. The steps below describe the whole liquidation process from first conversation to the company ceasing to exist, so that nothing about it comes as a surprise.

  1. Advice and nomination of a liquidator.The directors instruct a licensed insolvency practitioner, who advises on whether a creditors' voluntary liquidation is appropriate and prepares the paperwork
  2. Statement of Affairs.The directors set out, in a prescribed form, everything the company owns and everything it owes, naming every creditor and the amount due to each. This has to be accurate. It is the document creditors and the liquidator work from, and getting it wrong causes problems later
  3. Shareholders' resolution.The shareholders pass a resolution to wind the company up. This is the moment liquidation formally begins
  4. Creditors' decision.Creditors are given the Statement of Affairs and the opportunity to appoint a liquidator of their own choosing. In most cases they accept the practitioner the directors nominated
  5. Liquidator takes control.Company assets pass into the liquidator's hands. The directors' powers cease
  6. Asset realisation.The liquidator sells what can be sold and collects what is owed to the company
  7. Investigation and reporting.The liquidator reviews the conduct of every director and reports to the Insolvency Service
  8. Distribution and dissolution.Whatever is realised is distributed to creditors in the statutory order, and the company is dissolved and struck from the register at Companies House

From instruction to the shareholders' resolution is often two to three weeks. The liquidation itself usually runs six to twelve months, though cases involving property, disputed creditor claims or an investigation take longer. The company stops trading almost immediately, so the practical end of the business comes right at the start.

Where you sit in the queue

Distribution follows a statutory order that the liquidator cannot vary, and the full waterfall is set out on our company liquidation page. What matters here is where a director sits in it, because that is the part people get wrong.

If you lent money to your own company, you are an ordinary unsecured creditor, near the back, behind the liquidation costs, the employees, HMRC's preferential element and any floating charge. Directors regularly assume their loan comes back first because it was their money. It does not, and in most insolvent cases it does not come back at all. Anyone planning around recovering a director's loan should stop planning around it.

The other thing worth saying plainly is what suppliers will get, which is usually very little. Being straight with them about that is better than implying otherwise and being found out during the process.

What it means for you as a director

This is the part most directors are actually worried about, and the part thin guides skate over. Here is the honest position.

Your conduct is investigated. Always.

The liquidator has a statutory duty to review the conduct of every director and report to the Insolvency Service. This is not a sign that anything is wrong; it happens in every insolvent liquidation without exception. In the great majority of cases nothing further follows. Ordinary business failure, honestly handled and properly documented, is not what the regime is aimed at.

Wrongful trading is the real risk, and it is about timing

Wrongful trading exposure arises where a director carried on trading after the point where insolvent liquidation could no longer realistically be avoided, and the creditors ended up worse off for it. The test is not whether the company failed. It is what you knew, when you knew it, and what you did next. Directors who took advice early and kept records are in a considerably stronger position than those who pressed on and hoped.

Personal guarantees survive the liquidation

If you personally guaranteed company borrowing, a lease or a supplier account, liquidating the company does not extinguish that. The lender will look to you. Company directors are caught by this constantly, and a personal guarantee is the single most important thing to establish before you start rather than after. Dig out what you actually signed: guarantees hide in bank facilities, equipment leases, commercial property leases and supplier credit applications, and directors routinely forget the ones signed years ago. If a personal guarantee is called in and you cannot meet it, that becomes a personal debt problem with its own separate answers.

An overdrawn director's loan account becomes a debt you owe

Where a director's loan account is overdrawn, meaning more has come out over the years than went in, that balance is an asset of the company and the liquidator will pursue it as a debt you owe. Dividends declared when there were insufficient distributable profits can be reclassified this way too. Check this early, because it is often the largest single personal exposure and it is entirely foreseeable.

You can usually be a director again, but mind the name

Unless you are disqualified, you are generally free to be a director of another company. The trap is Section 216 of the Insolvency Act 1986, the restriction on re-use of company names, often called the phoenix company rules. For five years from the start of the liquidation you cannot be involved in a business using the liquidated company's name, or any name so similar it suggests a connection, unless one of the statutory exceptions applies. Breach is a criminal offence and can make you personally liable for the new company's debts. Company directors trip over this because the trading name feels like theirs to carry across. Take advice before registering anything that resembles the old name.

If a personal guarantee or an overdrawn loan account applies to you, the company procedure and your own finances are two halves of one problem. Our Debt Help Scotland section covers the personal side, including Scottish bankruptcy and trust deeds.

What your creditors see, and what they can do

A creditors' voluntary liquidation is a public process, and it is worth knowing exactly what becomes visible and when, because directors often imagine it is either more secret or more exposing than it really is.

The appointment is advertised in the Gazette, published as the Edinburgh Gazette for a Scottish company. Credit reference agencies pick it up from the Gazette, and the liquidation is recorded at Companies House against the company's filing history. Suppliers, customers and competitors can all see it. What is not published is the detail of your conduct report, which goes to the Insolvency Service and is not a public document.

As for what creditors can actually do, the answer is narrower than most directors fear.
They submit a claim
to the liquidator for what they are owed, and the liquidator adjudicates on it. Creditor claims that are disputed get examined rather than simply accepted
They can vote
on the choice of liquidator, and on certain decisions during the liquidation
They can ask questions
and request information about the conduct of the liquidation
They deal with the liquidator, not with you.
In practice claims are made in the liquidation rather than chased separately, and the liquidator takes over dealing with them. Worth knowing, though, that a creditors' voluntary liquidation carries no automatic court stay the way a compulsory winding up does: existing or new court action is only halted if the liquidator applies to the court for it, and a creditor keeps the right to petition to have the company wound up by the court instead. In practice few bother once a liquidator is in place
They cannot pursue you personally
for the company's debts, unless you gave a personal guarantee or there is a separate statutory ground, such as an overdrawn loan account, misfeasance, or trading under a prohibited name

That last pair is the point that gives most directors relief. The chasing stops. Creditor pressure that has been consuming your attention for months transfers to the insolvency practitioner on the day of appointment, and dealing with creditors stops being your job.

Creditors who behave differently

Not every creditor responds the same way, and knowing which one you are dealing with changes what is sensible to do before you liquidate.

HMRC

Frequently the largest creditor in a small limited company, and the one most likely to have petitioned had you not acted. HMRC is a preferential creditor for VAT, PAYE, employee National Insurance and CIS deductions, so it recovers ahead of trade creditors. It also looks closely at whether tax money was used to pay other creditors while the debt to it grew, which is one of the specific conduct issues the Insolvency Service takes an interest in.

A secured lender

A bank with a charge over company assets sits outside the general distribution and is paid from its security. In practice a secured creditor is often relaxed about the liquidation process itself, because its position is protected either way. What it will pursue is any personal guarantee you gave.

Your landlord

Commercial leases are among the most expensive liabilities in a liquidation, and the landlord becomes an unsecured creditor for the remaining rent. Landlords very commonly hold a director's personal guarantee, so check the lease before anything else.

Trade suppliers

Unsecured, at the back of the queue, and usually recovering little. Some will have retention of title clauses letting them reclaim goods they supplied but were never paid for, which is worth identifying early because those goods are not company assets to distribute.

You, as a creditor

If you lent the company money, you are an unsecured creditor too. That ranks behind almost everyone. Directors who expect to recover a loan they made to their own limited company are nearly always disappointed, and it is better to know that at the outset.

None of this changes whether you should liquidate. It changes what you check first, and how surprised you are by what follows.

One point that applies to every creditor equally: once the liquidation process begins, no creditor can improve its position by shouting louder. The order of payment is fixed by statute, and a creditor that has been aggressive gets exactly the same treatment as one that has been patient. Directors carrying months of pressure from a particular creditor sometimes find that the most useful thing about the whole insolvency process.

Telling your staff

Redundancies happen on appointment, and what employees can claim from the Redundancy Payments Service is the same in any insolvent liquidation, so the detail sits on our company liquidation page. What is particular to a CVL is that you control the timing, which means you can handle this conversation properly instead of your staff learning about it when the locks change.

Use that. Tell people before the appointment, not after. Have the practitioner's contact details and the claim reference process ready for them on the day, because the first thing anyone asks is how they get paid and when. Directors who manage this badly carry it for years afterwards, and it is one of the few parts of the process where acting early makes a human difference rather than merely a financial one.

One thing directors miss: if you are employed by your own company as well as being a director, you may be able to claim from the Redundancy Payments Service too.

What the directors actually have to do

Directors sometimes picture the liquidation process as something that simply happens to them once an insolvency practitioner is instructed. It is more involved than that, and knowing the obligations up front avoids the impression of reluctance that a conduct report will notice.

Hand over the books and records.
All of them, including the accounting system logins, and promptly. Missing or disorganised records are the single most common conduct criticism of company directors
Produce an accurate picture of the assets and the debt.
Every creditor named, every asset disclosed, including anything sold or transferred beforehand. The look-back is longer than most directors expect: in Scotland a gift or an undervalue transfer to a connected person can be unwound up to five years back, two years for anyone else, and a payment that favoured one creditor up to six months back
Explain the transactions the liquidator asks about.
Payments to connected parties, unusual disposals, and any debt repaid to one creditor ahead of others will be looked at, and a straightforward explanation given early is worth a great deal
Stop trading, and stop using the company's assets.
Company assets belong to the estate from the moment of appointment, and continuing to use a vehicle or equipment because it "was always mine in practice" causes real problems
Co-operate with the Insolvency Service
if it follows up on the conduct report

None of this is onerous for an insolvent company that has been run honestly. It is only difficult where records are poor, which is why the state of your bookkeeping matters more to your personal position than most directors expect.

What it costs, and what if there is no money

Fees come out of the company's assets, so where there is something to realise a creditors' voluntary liquidation often costs the directors nothing personally. Creditors, the liquidation committee or the court approve those fees, and the practitioner should set out the work they anticipate and what it is expected to cost, and explain the basis on which they are charging, before that basis is approved. Scotland works differently from England here: the fees estimate rule English guides describe does not apply, and the amount is formally determined after each accounting period rather than fixed at the start.

The harder case is a company with no assets, where someone has to fund the work and directors are usually the ones asked. This is the point at which some decide to do nothing and let the company drift instead. That is almost always the worse choice. Left alone, the company is eventually wound up by the court through compulsory liquidation: a less orderly process, no say in who is appointed, and a conduct report written against a background of inaction rather than cooperation. The fee saved is rarely worth what the loss of control costs.

If funding is the obstacle, say so early. There are options, and they are worth hearing before you decide to walk away.

CVL compared with the alternatives

CVL or compulsory liquidation

Both end the company. The difference is control. In a creditors' voluntary liquidation you choose the timing, you nominate the insolvency practitioner, and the record shows you dealt with the position. In compulsory liquidation a creditor petitions the court, the court picks the interim liquidator, and everything happens on someone else's schedule. If a winding up petition has already been served, the window for choosing is closing quickly.

CVL or CVA

A CVA keeps the company alive and repays creditors over time. A CVL closes it. The only question that matters is whether there is a viable business underneath the debt. If there is, a CVA is worth exploring properly. If there is not, a CVA delays the inevitable while adding cost.

CVL or administration

Administration is protective. It creates a moratorium while a rescue, a sale or a better return is arranged, and it makes sense where the business or its assets are worth more sold as a going concern than broken up. Administration is also the route where a sale to a buyer is already in prospect, through a pre-pack administration. Where nothing can be rescued and there is no going concern to sell, a creditors' voluntary liquidation is the more direct answer for an insolvent company, and a cheaper one.

CVL or striking off

Directors sometimes ask whether they can simply apply to strike the company off instead. Where real debts exist, the answer is no in practice: a creditor who objects can block the application, and a company struck off while insolvent can be restored to the register afterwards so that a liquidator can look at it properly. You end up in the same place, later, having appeared to avoid it. Strike off suits a dormant company with nothing owed, which is not the situation this page describes.
