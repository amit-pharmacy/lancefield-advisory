Protected trust deeds

In Scotland, and nowhere else in the UK, you can deal with unmanageable debt by granting a trust deed. You transfer your estate to a trustee, who realises what can be realised, takes contributions from your disposable income for an agreed period, and distributes the proceeds among your creditors. At the end of it the balance of the debts covered by the deed is written off. Where it becomes protected, and most do, the creditors bound by it can no longer pursue you or take diligence against you.

Protected trust deeds are a real and useful remedy, and also the debt solution most heavily marketed to people in Scotland, because arranging one earns a fee. Both things are true at once. The purpose of this page is to explain what it actually does, and to be honest about the two situations in which something else would serve you better.

Is a trust deed actually the right answer for you?

We arrange nothing. Nobody here becomes your trustee, we take no fee from a trust deed, and we are not paid by anyone who does. That is why this section comes first rather than last.

It may suit you if

You have unsecured debts of at least £5,000, which is the statutory minimum, typically credit cards, loans and overdrafts, and you genuinely cannot repay them in a reasonable time. You have disposable income you could make contributions from each month, and your circumstances are stable enough to keep those payments up for the full term. You want the creditor pressure to stop, and you accept that some of what you own may have to be realised to pay them.

Something else may suit you better

If you could repay the debt in full given time and protection from interest, the Debt Arrangement Scheme offers a Debt Payment Programme that does exactly that and writes nothing off, which means it does not touch your assets in the same way. If you have almost no disposable income and few assets, sequestration may be quicker and cleaner, and you would be discharged sooner. And if the debt is a company debt rather than yours, the answer may be on the insolvency practitioner side entirely.

If you have been recommended a protected trust deed by someone who would be paid to arrange it, and nobody mentioned a Debt Payment Programme, ask why. It is a fair question and any reasonable source of debt advice will have a reasonable answer. If a creditor is already taking action against you, ask about a moratorium too, because that buys time to get proper debt advice before you commit to anything.

How a trust deed becomes protected

This is the part that matters, and it is the part the marketing tends to skip.

Signing a trust deed does not by itself stop creditors taking action. What it creates initially is an ordinary trust deed, and that binds nobody except the creditors who sign up to it. The trustee registers it in the Register of Insolvencies and must send the paperwork to every known creditor within seven days. Creditors then have five weeks from registration to object. The deed becomes protected unless objections come in from a majority of them in number, or from creditors holding at least a third of the debt by value. Once protected, it binds every creditor covered by it, including those who never replied, and they can no longer take diligence against you for those debts. One narrow exception is worth knowing: a creditor who was never notified, or who objected in time, keeps a limited right to ask the court for your sequestration instead, and the court will only grant it if that is genuinely in the creditors' best interests.

If too many object, the deed does not become protected. It can still run as an ordinary trust deed with whoever signed up, but everyone else is free to continue their action against you, including going to court, and in practice a failed protection means reconsidering the whole approach.

The trustee must be a licensed insolvency practitioner, and the arrangement is supervised by the Accountant in Bankruptcy, Scotland's insolvency regulator, which also maintains the public Register of Insolvencies where the deed is recorded. Note one consequence of signing: while the trust deed subsists you cannot apply for your own sequestration, so it is a decision that closes a door as well as opening one.

What happens to your home

This is the first question almost everyone asks, and the honest answer is that it depends on your equity, not on the trust deed itself.

Your interest in your home forms part of the estate conveyed to the trustee. Your mortgage itself sits outside the trust deed and you keep paying it as normal, but the equity above the mortgage is what the trustee looks at. Where there is little or none, it is common for arrangements to be made that let you keep the property, sometimes by a third party paying the value of your interest, and sometimes because the equity is not worth realising. Where there is significant equity, the trustee has a duty to creditors to realise it, and that can mean the property has to be sold or the equity bought out. Where an agreement is reached to keep it, the payments for the equity typically run on after the four-year period rather than being found at the start. There is also a formal route, agreed before the deed is granted, that keeps the house outside the estate altogether where the lender agrees not to claim, and it requires a proper valuation by a chartered surveyor first.

What you should not do is sign anything before this has been worked through with a valuation and a redemption figure in front of you. An arrangement that looks affordable monthly can still end with the sale of a house, and finding that out in year three is not the same as knowing it in month one. If keeping the property is your priority, say so at the outset, because it may change which option is right.

The rest of what it affects

Your credit rating.
The trust deed is recorded in the Register of Insolvencies, which is public and searchable, and credit reference agencies pick it up, so it appears on your credit file. The effect on your credit rating lasts years beyond the end of the arrangement, and it is one of the reasons a Debt Payment Programme suits some people better
Your vehicle.
A car of modest value that you need for work is often retained; a valuable one is an asset like any other. Where the vehicle is on hire purchase it does not belong to you in the first place, and the agreement has to be dealt with separately
Your job.
For most people, nothing changes. A minority of professions and roles have restrictions or notification requirements, and certain positions become difficult. Check your contract and any professional body rules before you sign, not after
Being a company director.
A trust deed does not itself disqualify you, which surprises people. Sequestration is different: it is a criminal offence for an undischarged bankrupt to act as a director or take part in running a company without the court's permission. What a trust deed may do is trigger a term in the company's own articles of association, which under the standard Model Articles remove a director automatically where a composition is made with their creditors generally, or breach a shareholders' agreement, a lending covenant or an employment contract. Read the articles first
Windfalls during the term.
An inheritance, a redundancy payment, a lottery win or a compensation award received while the deed runs is generally claimed for the creditors
New credit.
The trust deed will restrict you from taking on credit during the arrangement, including credit cards, and any change in your income has to be reported

The trustee, the term and the money

The trustee is not your adviser. Their duty is to the creditors as a body, and their job is to realise what can be realised and distribute it. That is not a criticism, it is the design of the thing, and it is precisely why having someone independent explain the options to you beforehand matters.

You make contributions from your disposable income for the agreed term, which for most protected trust deeds is four years. The payments are set from a proper assessment of what you actually have left after reasonable living costs, including your mortgage or rent, using the Common Financial Statement, which is the statutory assessment tool, rather than a figure someone thinks you can manage. Social security benefits cannot be counted towards a contribution. If your circumstances change materially, the contributions can be reviewed in either direction, and you are discharged at the end.

A few debts survive the discharge whatever happens: student loans, court fines and penalties, compensation orders, aliment, and anything you incurred through fraud or breach of trust. A secured lender also keeps its rights over the security. Everything else covered by the deed is written off at the end.

The trustee's fees and outlays come out of the money collected, before the creditors are paid. That is normal and it is disclosed, but it is worth understanding: a proportion of what you pay in each month funds the administration rather than reducing your debt. Ask for the fee basis in writing before you sign.

What happens if you cannot keep it up

This is the risk the marketing does not lead with, and it is the reason the affordability assessment at the start matters so much.

If you stop paying, the trustee will first look at whether the contributions can be varied. Where the payments genuinely cannot be sustained, the trust deed can fail, though since 2024 there is a middle path: where circumstances have changed and there is no realistic prospect of resuming, the trustee can apply for an early discharge instead of letting the deed collapse. A failed trust deed is the worst of both worlds: the money you have paid in is gone, a good deal of it into fees, the debts revive to the extent they have not been paid, and your creditors are free to pursue you again. The trustee may also petition for your sequestration.

So the affordability figure at the outset should be conservative and should assume that some months will be worse than the one you are in now. An arrangement that only works if nothing goes wrong for four years is not an arrangement, it is a hope.

Trust deed, sequestration or DAS

Trust deed

Voluntary, four years for most people, and you are discharged from the remaining debt at the end. Your assets, including any equity in your home above the mortgage, are available to the trustee. Suits someone with real disposable income and debt they cannot clear in a sensible time.

Sequestration

Scotland's bankruptcy. Usually shorter, often cheaper to enter, and administered by a trustee who may be the Accountant in Bankruptcy. Being made bankrupt carries a wider public record and more restrictions while it lasts, and you are discharged sooner. For someone with minimal income and few assets it is frequently the more honest answer.

Debt Arrangement Scheme

A Debt Payment Programme that freezes interest and charges and stops creditor action while you repay in full over a longer period. Nothing is written off, which is why providers promote it least, and nothing is realised, which is why it is often the right answer for someone with equity in a home and a mortgage they want to keep.

The choice between them turns on three things: how much disposable income you really have, how much equity sits in your assets after the mortgage, and whether repaying the debt in full over a longer period is realistic. Anyone recommending one without having established all three is guessing.

If the debt came from a business

This is where most debt advice stops and where a large share of our work begins.

If you are a company director who signed a personal guarantee, or a sole trader, or a partner in a firm, you have two problems that have to be solved together. The company or the firm has its own position, and you have yours, and the order in which they are dealt with changes the outcome. Settling the personal side before the business position is resolved can leave you having paid for a liability that would have reduced. Dealing with the business first without understanding your guarantee exposure can leave you facing a claim you had not planned for.

Our creditors' voluntary liquidation and partnership liquidation pages cover the business side. What matters here is that the two are looked at as one problem by one adviser, which is the thing a debt-advice firm and an insolvency firm each do half of.
