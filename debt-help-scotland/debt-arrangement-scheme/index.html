Debt help in Scotland

The Debt Arrangement Scheme, and why you may not have been offered it

The Debt Arrangement Scheme is Scotland's statutory way of paying back what you owe at a rate you can actually manage. It is run by the Accountant in Bankruptcy on behalf of the Scottish Government. It freezes interest and charges, it stops your creditors enforcing against you, and it leaves your home and your other assets alone. You repay the debt in full, over a longer period than your original agreements allowed.

What a debt payment programme is

The scheme is the framework. The thing you actually apply for is a debt payment programme, usually shortened to DPP. Your money adviser works out what you can pay once your reasonable living costs are covered, and that single monthly payment is divided among your creditors until the debts inside the programme are cleared.

Interest and charges stop.
Interest, fees and penalties on the debts in the programme are frozen from the date of your application, and written off once you complete it
Enforcement stops.
Creditors in the programme cannot begin or carry on diligence, the Scottish term for enforcement such as an earnings arrestment or a bank arrestment
Your assets stay yours.
Nothing is transferred to a trustee, nothing has to be sold, and there is no assets test to pass
The debt is repaid in full.
Nothing is written off except the interest and charges, and that is the trade for keeping what you own

A DPP is not an insolvency procedure. You are not made bankrupt, no trustee takes control of anything, and you keep the running of your own money. That distinction matters more than it sounds, and it is most of the reason the scheme exists.

What happens to your home

Nothing, directly. This is the largest practical difference between the Debt Arrangement Scheme and everything else on the menu, and for a homeowner it is usually the whole argument. There is no trustee, so your share of the property is not part of an estate, no equity has to be released, and nobody asks you to sell or to remortgage.

Two honest caveats. Your mortgage and your rent are ongoing liabilities rather than debts to be included, so those payments carry on as normal. And if you are already behind, a lender or a landlord can still take steps to recover possession, because a programme protects you against enforcement of the debts inside it rather than against everything at once. Mortgage arrears can go into the programme, which often helps a great deal, but it does not remove the lender's underlying rights.

Set that against sequestration, where a home you own becomes part of the estate a trustee administers, or against a protected trust deed, where equity in the property usually has to be dealt with before the deed is granted. For anyone with something to lose, the difference is not a technicality.

You cannot apply on your own, and that is deliberate

Every application to the Debt Arrangement Scheme goes through an approved money adviser. This is not an administrative preference. The money adviser assesses your income and expenditure, agrees the payment with you, lists the debts, and submits the application on your behalf.

Approved advisers sit in local authorities, in Citizens Advice Bureaux, in the free advice sector and in some commercial firms. Free debt advice funded by the Scottish Government is available from Citizens Advice Scotland, StepChange and National Debtline, and any of them can act as your money adviser at no cost to you. If free debt advice is all you need, we will tell you so and point you towards it.

A good money adviser will also tell you when a programme is the wrong answer. If the payment needed to clear the debt in a sensible period is beyond your income, no money adviser can make the arithmetic work, and the conversation has to turn to whether the debt needs writing down instead.

The moratorium that buys you six months

If enforcement is already moving, you or your adviser can ask the Accountant in Bankruptcy for a moratorium on diligence before anything else is decided. It gives you six months during which creditors cannot serve a charge for payment, begin or continue diligence, or petition for your bankruptcy. You are allowed one in any twelve month period, so it is not to be spent casually.

The moratorium ran for six weeks until coronavirus legislation lengthened it, and the six month period was then made permanent. A great deal of guidance still online says six weeks. If you have read that somewhere, look at the date on the page before you rely on it.

How your creditors approve it

Once the application has been submitted, your creditors are given 21 days to respond. A creditor that does not reply is deemed to have consented, which is a deliberate feature of the design: silence approves the programme rather than blocking it. Where enough creditors agree, or are treated as agreeing, the DAS Administrator approves the application and the programme is entered on the DAS Register.

If a creditor does object, the programme is not finished. The DAS Administrator can still approve it where the proposal is fair and reasonable, weighing what you can afford against what the objecting creditor wants. Objections tend to be about how long the programme runs rather than about the principle of it.

After approval you make one payment to a payments distributor, who splits it among the creditors in the programme. You stop dealing with each of them separately, which for most people is the point at which the phone stops ringing.

What it costs, and what happens if you slip

The costs of running the scheme come out of the payments you make rather than being added on top, so what you owe is what you pay. We do not quote figures, because they are set in regulations and they change. Your money adviser will show you the split before you commit to anything.

If your circumstances change, the programme can be varied. Payment breaks are available where your disposable income falls by half or more, for up to six months, and the deferred period is added to the end of the programme rather than treated as a failure. Payment breaks are one of the reasons a statutory programme is safer than an informal debt payment plan, where any concession is whatever your creditor feels like granting that month.

If payments simply stop, the programme can be revoked once the equivalent of two payments is outstanding. Revocation puts you back roughly where you began: interest and charges can be applied again, and creditors are free to enforce. That is why the figure has to be one you can hold for years, rather than the highest number you can bear in the week you sign up.

DAS, trust deeds and sequestration compared

Three statutory options, three different trades. Which one fits depends on whether the debt can realistically be repaid at all.

Debt payment programme

You repay everything you owe over a longer period. Interest is frozen, enforcement stops, and your home, your car and your savings are not at risk. It suits someone with a dependable income and debts that are large but not hopeless. Nothing is written off, so it takes longer than the alternatives do.

Protected trust deed

You pay a contribution for a set period, normally 48 months, and the unsecured balance left at the end is written off. Trust deeds are the Scottish answer to an IVA. A Scottish trust deed brings in a trustee, and equity in your home usually has to be addressed, which is where the comparison with a DPP turns for most homeowners.

Sequestration

Scottish bankruptcy. Debts are written off, but your assets vest in a trustee and a home you own is exposed. It is the right answer when the debt cannot be repaid, and the wrong one when it can.

Why you may not have been offered it

Debt management is an industry. A commercial debt management firm is regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority and is paid out of what its customers pay it, and a statutory programme pays a commercial adviser less than the alternatives do. That is not a scandal, it is an incentive, and it is worth understanding before you take advice from anybody who is also selling you something.

The effect is that DAS Scotland is under-advertised relative to how often it is the right answer, while a Scottish trust deed is advertised heavily. It is worth knowing what the informal version leaves out. A private debt management plan has no statutory force at all: interest is frozen only if every creditor agrees to it, enforcement is not stopped, and a payment plan of that kind can collapse the moment one creditor loses patience. A debt payment programme does all of it by law.

We have no debt management product, no panel and no referral fee to earn by steering you one way rather than another. If the answer is a programme arranged for nothing by a debt adviser at a charity, that is what we will say, and you will not hear from us again unless you want to.
