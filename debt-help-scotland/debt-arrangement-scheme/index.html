The Debt Arrangement Scheme

The Debt Arrangement Scheme, always shortened to DAS, is the Scottish Government's statutory way for Scottish residents to repay debt over a longer period without any of it being written off. You agree a Debt Payment Programme, you make a single affordable monthly payment, that money goes to a payment distributor who divides it among your creditors, and while the payment plan runs the interest and charges are frozen and your creditors cannot take diligence against you.

What a debt payment programme actually does

It freezes interest and charges.
From the day the application goes in, not from approval, interest and charges stop being payable, and once the programme completes they cease to be owed at all. For someone whose balances have been rising despite paying every month, this alone changes the arithmetic completely
It stops diligence.
Creditors covered by the programme cannot use earnings arrestment, bank arrestment or attachment against you, and cannot petition for your sequestration for those debts
It consolidates everything into one payment plan.
A single monthly payment to a payment distributor, who divides it among your creditors, instead of managing a dozen separate arrangements with credit cards, loans and catalogues
It repays in full.
The debt you owe on the day you apply is paid, over a longer period. The interest and charges that would otherwise have piled up are the only thing extinguished, and that is the trade for everything above. There is one long-stop: after twelve years, where seventy per cent of the original debt has been paid, the balance can be composed and discharged
It does not touch the home you live in.
There is no trustee and nothing is conveyed to anybody. A programme can carry a condition that you realise an asset, but the regulations expressly except the house or mobile home you live in, and since 2018 it can only be brought in at all if you choose to include it

That last point is the one that decides it for a great many people, and it is the difference that gets least airtime.

Who it suits, and who it does not

It suits you if

You have a reliable monthly surplus, you could clear the debt in full given enough time and no more interest, and you have something worth protecting: equity in a home, or simply a strong preference for keeping your credit rating from taking the harder hit a formal insolvency brings. It also suits people whose profession or employment makes an insolvency awkward.

It does not suit you if

The debt is so large relative to your income that repaying it in full would take an unreasonable period. There is a point past which a programme becomes a life sentence rather than a solution, and at that point a trust deed or sequestration, which do write debt off, is the more honest answer. It also does not suit someone whose income is too irregular to commit to a monthly figure.

The honest test is arithmetic. Take the total debt, divide it by what you can genuinely afford each month, and look at the number of years that produces. If the answer is a period you can live with, DAS is very likely your answer. If it is not, it is not, and no amount of preferring it will change that.

Why you may not have been told about it

Worth saying directly, because it explains a pattern people notice.

A Scottish trust deed generates a fee for the firm that arranges and administers it. Sequestration generates a fee too. A Debt Payment Programme is administered by an approved money adviser, and where those advisors sit in the free advice sector, as many do, there is no commercial fee at all. The result is entirely predictable: the option that pays least is mentioned least.

That is not an accusation of dishonesty against anyone. It is a structural observation about how debt advice is paid for in the United Kingdom, and it is why the Financial Conduct Authority banned debt packagers from taking referral fees altogether in 2023, having found the fees paid for trust deeds and IVAs were far higher than for anything else. It is also why our position on this page is what it is: we arrange nothing, and we have nothing to gain from which route you take.

How you get one

Speak to an approved money adviser.
Only an approved money adviser can submit a DAS application. Most of these debt advisers sit in local authorities, Citizens Advice Bureaux and other free-to-client services, and we can point you to one
Ask about the moratorium.
Applying for a moratorium gives you six months' protection from diligence while you get advice and put the application together, and you can use it once in any twelve months. It is the thing to do first if a creditor is already moving against you
Build the financial statement.
Income, reasonable living costs including your mortgage or rent, and what genuinely remains. This information is the whole application, and an over-optimistic figure here is what causes payment plans to fail later
The application goes to the DAS Administrator.
That role sits with the Accountant in Bankruptcy
Creditors are notified and may object.
A creditor who does not respond within twenty-one days is deemed to consent. Where creditors holding nine tenths by value consent, the programme is approved automatically. Where they do not, the DAS Administrator decides whether it is fair and reasonable, against prescribed criteria, and can approve it over an objection
The Debt Payment Plan starts.
You make one monthly payment to a payment distributor, who distributes it to your creditors, and the interest and charges stop

The application itself is free to you, and a money adviser cannot charge an individual a fee for this work at all. There is a fee within the scheme, taken from what is distributed: twenty per cent to the payments distributor and two per cent to the Accountant in Bankruptcy. Creditors receive seventy-eight pence in the pound rather than you paying anything extra.

Your home, and why this is the section that matters

Under a Debt Payment Programme nothing is conveyed to a trustee, because there is no trustee. Your home is not part of any estate and nobody has a duty to creditors to realise it. The Administrator does look at any equity in land you own when deciding whether a programme is fair and reasonable, but the regulations expressly except the property you live in from being realised. You keep paying your mortgage as normal, outside the programme, and the DAS deals with the unsecured debt: credit cards, loans, overdrafts and the like. Mortgage arrears themselves are treated differently and should be raised with the adviser separately.

Compare that with a Scottish trust deed, where your interest in the property forms part of the estate conveyed to the trustee, and significant equity has to be realised for the creditors, potentially by a sale. For a homeowner with equity, that difference is frequently worth more than everything else on this page combined.

This is the single most common reason we tell someone to go and look at DAS. If you own a home with equity in it and you have been recommended a trust deed, get a second view before you sign anything.

What can go wrong

Missing payments.
A programme can be revoked where arrears reach the equivalent of two months' payments. If it is revoked, the protection ends, the interest and charges that had been suspended become payable again back to the date you applied, the revocation itself counts as apparent insolvency, and you are back where you began with time lost
An over-optimistic budget.
The commonest cause of the above. The figure should assume that some months will be worse than this one
A change in income.
Payment plans can be varied where circumstances change, and a variation is far better than silence. Tell your debt adviser early
A creditor who will not engage.
The fair and reasonable test exists precisely for this, and an objection does not automatically defeat an application
The debt was never going to be repayable.
Where the arithmetic at the outset was wrong, a programme delays a decision rather than solving anything
Taking on new credit.
Unlike a trust deed, the credit restriction here is in the regulations rather than in an agreement, with narrow exceptions for things like emergency repairs and trade credit in the ordinary course of business

DAS, trust deed or sequestration

Debt Arrangement Scheme

Repay in full over longer. Interest and charges frozen, diligence stopped, no trustee, no assets realised, no insolvency, and a softer effect on your credit rating. Suits a reliable monthly payment and assets worth protecting.

[link: Trust deed]

A formal insolvency. Contribute for a set term, usually four years, then the balance is written off. Your estate including any equity in your home is conveyed to a trustee. Suits someone who cannot repay in full in a sensible period.

[link: Sequestration]

Scotland's bankruptcy. Usually the shortest route and often the cheapest to enter, with the widest public record and the most restrictions during it. Frequently the right answer for someone with minimal income and few assets.

All three stop creditor action. The differences that decide between them are whether debt is written off, whether your assets are realised, and how long you are committed for. Any recommendation made without weighing all three is not a recommendation, it is a default.

If the debt came from a business

DAS is for individuals habitually resident in Scotland, and it covers debts for which you are personally liable. A director who has given a personal guarantee, a sole trader and a partner in a firm can all use it for their own liabilities, and business debts such as HMRC arrears in a sole trade can be included where the liability is personal.

There is also a separate route for the firm itself, known as Business DAS. A partnership, a trust or an unincorporated body can have its own programme, provided a money adviser certifies that it can pay within five years and is still trading. A sole trader is an individual and uses ordinary DAS. Where you are a partner, a debt can go into your programme or the firm's, but not both.

What DAS does not do is deal with a limited company's own debts, which stay with the company and need the routes on our insolvency practitioner side. Where both exist, the sequence matters: settling your personal position before the company's is resolved can mean paying for a liability that would have reduced. That joined-up view is the thing neither a money adviser nor an insolvency firm usually provides on its own, and it is the reason this section of the site exists.
