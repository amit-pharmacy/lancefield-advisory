Debt help in Scotland

The Accountant in Bankruptcy, explained

The Accountant in Bankruptcy is the public body that runs personal insolvency in Scotland. It awards bankruptcy, acts as trustee in a large share of cases, supervises the trustee in the rest, keeps the public register of who is in a formal process, and administers the statutory repayment scheme. Most people meet the name for the first time on an envelope, and the letter inside rarely explains what the organisation actually is.

What the Accountant in Bankruptcy is

The Accountant in Bankruptcy, shortened to AiB almost everywhere including in its own correspondence, is an executive agency of the Scottish Government. It is part of government but runs at arm's length from ministers, with its own chief executive, its own budget and its own statutory duties. Those duties sit in the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act 2016, the main piece of law behind personal insolvency here, and in the legislation behind the statutory repayment scheme.

The name describes a person as well as an organisation. The Accountant in Bankruptcy is a statutory office holder appointed by Scottish Ministers, and the staff who write to you act under that office. The agency works out of Kilwinning in Ayrshire, and nearly all of its contact with the public happens by post, telephone and online form rather than face to face.

It holds two roles that pull against each other, which explains most of what follows. It regulates: it oversees the trustee in every Scottish personal insolvency case and can be asked to look at how that trustee is behaving. It also administers cases directly, and in a great many of them it is the trustee itself. The same body can therefore be your case handler, your trustee and the referee at once. That is not a criticism. It is worth knowing before you assume the person answering the telephone is on your side of the table.

What the AiB actually does

It awards bankruptcy.
Apply for your own bankruptcy and the decision is taken by the AiB rather than by a court. Where creditors want you made bankrupt against your will, they petition the sheriff court instead
It acts as trustee.
In a debtor application the AiB becomes trustee over your estate unless a licensed insolvency practitioner is named and agrees to take the job on
It supervises every other trustee.
An insolvency practitioner acting as trustee in a Scottish bankruptcy, or under a protected trust deed, reports to the AiB and accounts to it for the case
It runs the Debt Arrangement Scheme.
The AiB holds the separate office of DAS Administrator, approving each debt payment programme and overseeing the money moving through it
It keeps the Register of Insolvencies.
The public record of Scottish personal insolvency cases and of corporate insolvency appointments north of the border
It can grant a moratorium.
A short application to the AiB buys you a period of protection during which creditors cannot enforce against you while you take advice
It publishes the numbers.
Quarterly statistics on personal and corporate insolvency, and the Notes for Guidance that trustees and money advisers work from

Two of those matter far more than the others to somebody in difficulty: whether the AiB ends up as your trustee, and what ends up on the register.

When the AiB is your trustee

A trustee takes control of your estate, sells what can be sold, deals with creditors' claims and pays out whatever is available. Somebody has to do that job in every bankruptcy, and by default here it falls to the AiB.

The AiB acts as trustee wherever no other trustee is appointed. In a debtor application you may name an insolvency practitioner who is willing to act, and if you do, that person becomes your trustee. If you do not, or the nominated practitioner declines, the AiB takes the role. The AiB is also trustee in every Minimal Asset Process bankruptcy, the shorter route built for people on a low income with few assets. And where a trustee resigns, dies or is removed with nobody replacing them, the AiB picks up the case.

What that means in practice is worth being blunt about. Your trustee is not your adviser and is not acting for you. The trustee owes statutory duties to creditors, and those duties include investigating how the debt arose, asking about assets you may not have mentioned, and deciding whether you have income to spare. Being straight with the trustee from the first form is the most useful thing you can do, because everything is harder when the trustee is discovering things rather than being told them.

Creditors submit their claims to the trustee rather than to you. Once bankruptcy is awarded most creditors must stop contacting you directly and deal with the trustee instead, and they cannot continue diligence, the Scottish term for enforcement such as an earnings arrestment or a bank arrestment. Creditors do keep some rights. A secured lender still holds its security over your home. Creditors can object to your discharge if they believe you have not co-operated. And a few debts, court fines and child maintenance arrears among them, sit outside the process entirely, so those creditors can still pursue you.

Where you have surplus income, your trustee assesses it and asks for a monthly payment. That payment comes from a statutory calculation of what is left after reasonable living costs rather than from negotiation, and it runs for a set period. Benefits count as income in that assessment, which is why people whose income is mainly benefits are frequently assessed as having nothing to pay at all.

The Register of Insolvencies, and what shows on it

The AiB keeps that register, and it is genuinely public. Anyone with an internet connection can search it, free, without saying who they are or why they want to know. This is the part most people find hardest, and it is better faced now than discovered later.

An entry names you, gives an address, and records the date of the award, the trustee's name and contact details, and the date you are discharged. It does not list what you owe or to whom. A trust deed appears once it has become protected. Company appointments in Scotland appear too, which is how a supplier checks a customer.

In practice very few people are ever looked up. Credit reference agencies do check, and so do some lenders and some employers in regulated roles. Your neighbours almost certainly do not. The larger practical effect is on your credit rating: a bankruptcy sits on your credit file for years counted from the date it was awarded rather than from your discharge, and it makes borrowing harder and dearer while it is there. For most people the credit rating damage arrived long before, with the defaults and missed instalments that led here, so it is worth asking honestly how much of a good credit rating is genuinely left to protect.

Your bank account is a separate question and it comes up constantly. Bankruptcy does not automatically close a bank account, but banks often freeze or close one when they learn of it, particularly where the same bank is owed money. Opening a basic bank account elsewhere first, and getting wages and benefits paid into it, saves a great deal of trouble later.

How the AiB differs from the Insolvency Service

People assume the two are the same organisation under different names. They are not, and the differences matter if you have been reading English guidance and applying it to yourself.

The Insolvency Service is an executive agency of the UK government covering England and Wales, and its official receivers act in bankruptcies and compulsory liquidations there. It has no role in Scottish personal insolvency, and the AiB has none south of the border. The vocabulary differs too. What is an IVA in England is a protected trust deed here, and the statutory repayment scheme has no English counterpart at all.

Some differences are structural rather than cosmetic. In England and Wales a debtor's bankruptcy application goes to an adjudicator. Here it goes to the AiB. There is no official receiver in Scotland, because the AiB does that work itself. And the AiB does not license insolvency practitioners: they are authorised by professional bodies across the UK, and the AiB supervises how they run Scottish cases rather than granting the licence.

Corporate insolvency works the other way round. Liquidation and administration are largely GB-wide law, so a Scottish company closing follows broadly the same statute as an English one, with Scottish court procedure layered on top. The AiB appoints nobody to a company. A director facing a failed business and a personal shortfall is therefore dealing with two systems in the same month, which is exactly the situation this firm was built for.

Dealing with the AiB

Everything runs on your case reference.
Quote it on every letter, form and call, and keep copies of what you send
Most of your dealings are with your trustee, not a switchboard.
Where an insolvency practitioner holds the appointment, the AiB is supervising rather than handling your case day to day
Answer the forms fully and on time.
A statement of assets and liabilities that arrives half complete slows everything down and invites more questions
Report changes as they happen.
A new job, a drop in income, a move or an inheritance all have to be disclosed, and any payment can be reassessed in either direction
You can ask for a review.
Many AiB decisions can be reviewed on request, and from that review there is an appeal to the sheriff. The time limits are short, so raise it early rather than after taking advice
Complaints have their own route.
The AiB runs a complaints procedure, and a complaint it does not resolve can be taken to the Scottish Public Services Ombudsman

One more thing, and it is the source of most of the frustration we hear about. The AiB administers the process. It does not advise you on whether the process is right for you. Its staff cannot tell you whether a trust deed would protect your home better than bankruptcy, or whether you should be doing neither for another six months. The law requires you to see an approved money adviser before any debtor application in any event, and free advice is available from Citizens Advice, StepChange and National Debtline.

Where the AiB fits among your options

Nearly everyone who reaches this page is really working out which of three formal routes applies to them. The AiB touches all three.

Bankruptcy, or sequestration

The formal name is sequestration, though bankruptcy remains the ordinary Scottish word and the AiB uses both. Assets are realised, creditors receive what there is, and the balance is written off. The AiB is either your trustee or the supervisor of the practitioner who is. Under the Minimal Asset Process it is always the trustee.

Protected trust deed

A voluntary arrangement with your creditors, administered by an insolvency practitioner and supervised by the AiB rather than run by it. You pay a contribution for a set period and the remaining unsecured balance is written off at the end, provided the deed became protected and you kept to its terms. Our page on trust deeds explains how protection is obtained.

Debt Arrangement Scheme

A statutory repayment programme. You repay the debt in full over a longer period, interest and charges are frozen, creditors cannot enforce, and nothing is written off because nothing needs to be. The AiB approves the programme in its DAS Administrator role. See Debt Arrangement Scheme.

If the debt belongs to your company rather than to you, the AiB is not the body you need. A winding up petition already served, a director-led CVL, or a partnership insolvency where the partners carry personal liability are corporate questions first and personal ones second. The rest of Debt Help Scotland covers the personal side, and the two are usually best dealt with together rather than one after the other.
