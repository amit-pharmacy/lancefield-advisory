Debt help in Scotland

Sequestration in Scotland, explained without judgment

Sequestration is the Scottish term for bankruptcy. It is a formal process that writes off debts you have no realistic prospect of repaying, and it exists because sometimes a situation cannot be fixed by paying more slowly.

Your home, your car, your job

These are the three questions almost everyone asks first, so here they are first.

Your home

Your share of any property you own becomes part of the estate the trustee deals with, so a home you own is at risk. That does not automatically mean it is sold. Much depends on how much equity there is, who else owns it, and whether the mortgage payments are being maintained. Keeping up mortgage payments matters, because the lender's security survives the sequestration. If you rent, sequestration does not usually affect your tenancy directly, though your landlord may treat rent arrears as a debt included in the process. Two things are worth knowing. Your trustee cannot sell a family home without the consent of your spouse, civil partner or former spouse, or the sheriff's authority, and the sheriff can refuse a sale or postpone it for up to three years. And if the trustee has taken no step against your interest in the home within three years of the sequestration, it comes back to you, but that clock only starts on time if you tell the trustee about the property within three months of the award, so tell them. This is the single most important thing to take proper advice on before applying.

Your car

A car of modest value that you need for work or caring responsibilities can often be kept. A high value vehicle is more likely to be sold, or you may be asked to pay its value into the estate. Cars on finance are treated differently again, because the vehicle may not legally be yours yet.

Your job

Most people keep their jobs. Some professions and roles have restrictions, so it is worth checking your employment contract and any professional body rules. You cannot act as a company director, or be involved in forming or managing a company, while sequestrated without the court's permission.

What sequestration actually is

Sequestration transfers your assets to a trustee, who sells what can be sold and distributes the proceeds among your creditors. In return, the debts included are written off at the end of the process, whether or not they were paid in full.

It is administered by the Accountant in Bankruptcy, the Scottish Government agency responsible for personal insolvency in Scotland. The trustee is either the Accountant in Bankruptcy or a licensed insolvency practitioner appointed to act. Where an insolvency practitioner is appointed as trustee, they take on statutory duties to your creditors as well as responsibility for administering your estate, which is why the role has to be held by someone licensed and regulated.

Sequestration is recorded on the Register of Insolvencies, which is public. It also affects your credit rating: the sequestration appears on your credit file and will make borrowing harder and more expensive for several years. For many people the credit rating damage has already been done by missed payments and defaults long before they reach this point, which is worth weighing honestly rather than treating a good credit rating as something still to protect.

This is a Scottish process under Scottish law. It is not the same as bankruptcy in England and Wales, and the alternatives are different too. A protected trust deed is the Scottish equivalent of an IVA, and the Debt Arrangement Scheme has no English counterpart at all. If you have been reading English guidance and applying it to your own position, some of it will not hold here.

The two routes into sequestration

Minimal Asset Process (MAP)

Sometimes called MAP bankruptcy, this is the simpler, cheaper route for people on a low income with few assets and no property. The application fee is £50, and it is waived if you are on a qualifying benefit or assessed as having no surplus income, which covers almost everyone eligible for MAP in the first place. The process is shorter, and there is less trustee involvement. To qualify you must owe no more than £25,000 in total, own assets worth no more than £2,000 with no single item worth more than £1,000, and own no land. A vehicle you reasonably need is left out of that total if it is worth £3,000 or less, and student loans do not count towards the £25,000. There is no minimum debt: the old £1,500 floor was removed in February 2023. You also need either an assessment showing you can pay nothing towards your debts, or six months of qualifying benefits with no other income. You cannot use MAP if you have been sequestrated in the last five years, or used MAP in the last ten. Discharge comes automatically six months after your bankruptcy is awarded, unless the Accountant in Bankruptcy takes you out of MAP first, which it can do if your assets turn out to be worth more than the limit, if you are assessed as able to contribute, or if you do not co-operate.

Full administration sequestration

The standard route, used where MAP does not apply, typically because you own property, have assets of some value, or have surplus income. A trustee is appointed to realise assets and administer the estate, and discharge is a decision taken on the trustee's report rather than something that happens by itself.

You can apply for your own sequestration, and most people do. Whichever route you use, the law requires you to see an approved money adviser first. That is a statutory precondition rather than a formality, and in practice the adviser is the one who submits your application. For MAP you also need a Certificate for Sequestration from that adviser. For full administration you need the certificate, or to meet the test for apparent insolvency, or to have granted a trust deed that creditors would not let become protected. You also need to owe at least £3,000. A creditor owed above a set amount can also petition for your sequestration, but only if you are apparently insolvent as well. Owing the money is not enough on its own.

What happens during the process

  • You apply through the Accountant in Bankruptcy, or a creditor petitions the court
  • A trustee is appointed to take control of your estate
  • Assets that can be realised are identified and sold, with certain essentials protected
  • If you have surplus income, you will normally be asked to make payments under a Debtor Contribution Order, assessed from what is left after reasonable living costs
  • Creditors submit claims and are paid from whatever the trustee realises
  • You are discharged, and the included debts are written off
  • Any contribution order can continue after discharge, so the payments and the discharge are two separate things

Under MAP, discharge is automatic at six months. In a full administration sequestration it can come from twelve months onwards, on the trustee's report rather than automatically. Either way the trustee's work on the estate can carry on beyond your discharge, and contributions may continue past it.

What your creditors can and cannot do

This is usually the part that brings the most immediate relief, because the pressure people are living under comes from creditors rather than from the debt itself.

Once you are sequestrated, most creditors must stop contacting you directly and must deal with your trustee instead. They cannot continue diligence, which is the Scottish term for enforcement action such as earnings arrestment or bank arrestment, in respect of the debts included. Ongoing interest and charges on those debts stop. Creditors who want a share of whatever the trustee realises have to submit a formal claim rather than chase you for payment.

Creditors keep some rights. A secured creditor such as your mortgage lender retains its security over the property, so it can still repossess if the mortgage payments are not maintained. A creditor can object to your discharge if it believes you have not cooperated. And any creditor owed a debt that is not covered by sequestration, such as a court fine, can still pursue you for it.

Unsecured creditors, meaning credit cards, personal loans, overdrafts and most trade creditors, generally rank equally and share whatever is left after the costs of the process and any preferential claims. In many cases unsecured creditors receive very little, which is precisely why sequestration writes off the balance.

There is also a step you can take before deciding anything. A moratorium gives you six months of protection from diligence while you take advice and choose a route. It is entered on the Register of Insolvencies, and a money adviser can apply for it.

Which debts are included, and which are not

Most unsecured debt is included and written off: credit cards, personal loans, overdrafts, catalogue debt, most utility arrears, council tax arrears and, in many cases, business debts you are personally liable for. If a debt is unsecured and it existed at the date of sequestration, the working assumption is that it is included.

Some are not. Student loans, court fines, compensation orders and debts arising from fraud or breach of trust are not written off. Child maintenance is a special case: arrears that built up before the date of sequestration are written off, but maintenance falling due from that date onwards is not, and the Child Maintenance Service can still act on it. Secured debts such as your mortgage are treated differently, because the lender's security over the property survives.

If you have signed a personal guarantee for a business, the guaranteed debt is a personal debt, and it can be included. For directors and sole traders whose business and household finances have become tangled together, this is the point where the two sides of the problem have to be looked at as one. That crossover is a large part of what we do.

The alternatives, which usually deserve a look first

Sequestration is the most serious option, not the default. In Scotland there are two formal alternatives and several informal ones.

Protected trust deed

You pay a monthly contribution worked out by a statutory assessment of what you can afford, normally for 48 months from the date the deed is granted. It can run longer if you miss payments, or if you agree to keep paying beyond that, which is common where there is equity in your home. At the end your trustee applies for you to be discharged and the remaining unsecured debt is written off, though that depends on the deed having become protected and on you keeping to the terms. Your home is treated differently than in sequestration, and for homeowners this is often the more protective route.

Trust deeds

Debt Arrangement Scheme (DAS)

A statutory payment programme that lets you repay the debt itself in full over a longer period, with interest and charges frozen from the day you apply and creditors of the debts in the programme barred from taking enforcement action. Complete it and the frozen interest and charges are written off; if it fails they can be added back. Your home is protected, though a programme can be approved on condition that you sell some other asset. If you can realistically clear the debt given time, this is usually the better answer.

If the debt is your company's, not yours

Directors often arrive here because a business has failed and a personal guarantee has been called in. If that is your position, the company side needs its own answer, whether that is liquidation, a CVA, or dealing with a winding up petition that has already been served. Sorting the personal position while leaving the company unresolved rarely works, because the guarantee keeps generating new liability.

There are also informal arrangements, and sometimes the right answer is simply a repayment plan and some breathing space. Free debt advice is available in Scotland from Citizens Advice Scotland, StepChange and National Debtline, and a money adviser or debt adviser at any of them can issue the certificate you need to apply. Common questions

Frequently asked questions

It depends which route you are in. Under the Minimal Asset Process you are discharged automatically six months after your bankruptcy is awarded. In a full administration sequestration the Accountant in Bankruptcy can discharge you from twelve months onwards, but that is a decision based on your trustee's report rather than an automatic date, and it can be refused or delayed if you have not co-operated. Contributions from surplus income can continue beyond discharge, and the Accountant in Bankruptcy's guidance is that it stays on your credit file for six years.

Sequestration is entered on the Register of Insolvencies, which is a public register. In practice, most people's friends, neighbours and colleagues never look at it. Your employer is not notified as a matter of course unless your role or profession requires it.

That is what the Minimal Asset Process exists for. The £50 application fee is waived for anyone on a qualifying benefit or assessed as having no surplus income, so in practice it costs nothing; there is less trustee involvement, and discharge comes automatically at six months. The same waiver applies to the £150 fee on the full route, so do not rule that out on cost alone. It is built for people on a low income without property, and since the minimum debt requirement was removed in 2023 there is no longer any floor to fall below. We can check whether you fall inside the limits.

Sequestration does not stop you claiming welfare benefits. Your benefit income is taken into account when the trustee assesses whether you have surplus income to contribute, and in many cases people whose income is mainly from benefits are assessed as having nothing to pay. Benefit overpayments are treated as debts, and whether a particular overpayment is written off depends on how it arose, so it is worth listing them when you take advice.

Most sequestrations are applied for by the debtor rather than forced by a creditor, so yes in that sense. But you cannot lodge the application yourself. Seeing an approved money adviser comes first and is required by law rather than merely advisable, and it is that adviser who submits it for you. For MAP you need a Certificate for Sequestration from them; for full administration you need the certificate or must meet the test for apparent insolvency. You do not need a solicitor.

You cannot act as a company director or manage a company without the court's permission. You must tell a lender you are sequestrated if you are seeking credit of £2,000 or more, and for credit of any amount at all while your debts total £1,000 or more. You must cooperate fully with your trustee, including disclosing assets and any change in your circumstances. Failing to cooperate can extend the restrictions.

Your partner's own debts and their sole assets are not included. Jointly owned assets and joint debts are affected, and if you own a home together their position needs looking at carefully. A joint bank account is likely to be frozen at the outset.

Not automatically, but you will almost certainly stop being a director of it. Acting as a director while sequestrated is a criminal offence without the court's permission, and most companies' articles remove a director from office the moment they are prohibited from acting. Permission from the court does not put you back on the board; the company has to appoint you again. Where the company is also insolvent, the two problems are best dealt with together rather than one at a time.
