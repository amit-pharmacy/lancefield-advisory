Fee estimates and the basis of remuneration

In a formal insolvency you will be sent a document setting out what the office holder expects to charge. It is worth reading properly. The fee comes out of the estate, the same pot that would otherwise be shared among creditors.

Why there is no Lancefield estimate here

We take no insolvency appointments. Nobody here becomes your liquidator, administrator, receiver or trustee, so there is no estate we are paid from and no fee of ours to estimate. The estimate that matters comes from the licensed insolvency practitioner holding the appointment, and it should reach you before you are asked to approve anything.

Reading someone else's fee estimate is easier to do honestly when none of your own income depends on appointments.

What you should be told before a fee is approved

The standard is Statement of Insolvency Practice 9 (Scotland). It applies to a creditors' voluntary liquidation, an administration and a company voluntary arrangement alike, though solvent members' voluntary liquidation work sits outside it. Anyone approving a fee should have enough to judge whether it is reasonable:

The work proposed.
What is intended, and why each part of it is necessary
What it will cost.
And whether it benefits creditors or is simply required by statute
The rates.
Where time is charged, rates by grade of staff and the minimum time unit
The likely return.
What creditors can expect to receive
The expenses.
What comes out of the estate on top of the fee, and any work sent outside

The three bases, and what the estimate binds

Remuneration is fixed on one of three bases, or a combination: a percentage of the assets realised, the time properly given, or a set amount. Different bases can apply to different parts of the work, which is common where a company later moves into liquidation.

An estimate is not a suggestion. In England and Wales an office holder cannot draw more than the total in it without further approval. Scotland reaches the same protection differently: outlays and remuneration are determined after the event, period by period, against what was estimated.

Outlays, expenses and disbursements

Scottish rules say outlays for what is spent from the estate other than the fee itself. SIP 9 says expenses, and treats disbursements as a subset: payments the office holder meets first and is reimbursed for later.

Category 1.
Payments to a firm other than the office holder's own for the service concerned, such as advertising, insurance or storage. No prior approval needed
Category 2.
Payments to an associate, or any payment carrying a shared or allocated element. These need approval before payment, in the same way the fee does

A charge calculated as a percentage of the remuneration, an administration fee on top of it, and overheads belonging inside the charge out rates are all disallowed.

Who approves the fee, and how to challenge it

In a Scottish liquidation the liquidator claims for each accounting period and the liquidation committee determines what is payable, or the court where no committee exists. In an administration the creditors' committee determines it, or the creditors by decision procedure.

If you disagree there are two routes. A committee determination in a liquidation can be appealed to the court within 14 days, on notice, by the liquidator or any creditor or contributory. Separately, creditors representing at least 25 per cent in value can apply to have outlays or remuneration reduced as, in all the circumstances, excessive, with an eight week deadline in an administration.

Before either, ask. A request for more information is meant to be handled fairly and in proportion to the case, and one good question often does more than an application. The same goes for a pre-pack administration or an LPA receivership.
