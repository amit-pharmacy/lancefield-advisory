Creditor Services

Details of our work

When an office holder asks creditors to approve what they are paid, or reports on what has happened since the last time, the professional standards require more than a figure. They require a narrative: an explanation of the work actually done, why it was necessary, what it achieved, and what remains. This page explains what that narrative should contain and how to read one.

What you should receive, and when

An office holder reports to creditors at intervals through the case, and again at the end. The reports are cumulative: each one covers the period since the last, so read together they should account for the whole of the appointment.

An account of the period.
What was done, by whom, and what it produced. Not a list of activities, but an explanation of what the estate got for the time spent
What has been realised.
Assets recovered and money received, against what was expected at the outset
What has been paid out.
Costs, expenses, and any distributions, with the office holder's remuneration shown separately
What remains.
Outstanding matters, why they are outstanding, and what the office holder expects to achieve
Progress against the estimate.
Where an estimate of remuneration was given, how the actual position compares to it. Our fee estimates page explains that in Scotland the estimate is not a cap: outlays and remuneration are determined after each accounting period by the committee, the creditors or the court
The creditors' position.
What has been distributed, or an honest statement of the prospects of a distribution

Where the case has moved from one procedure to another, for instance an administration ending in a creditors' voluntary liquidation, the reporting picks up in the new procedure and the earlier office holder accounts for their own period.

How to tell a useful report from a boilerplate one

This is the practical part, and it is the reason a page like this is worth writing properly.

Does it explain, or does it list?
"Correspondence with creditors, 14 hours" is an activity. "Dealt with 60 creditor claims, of which 11 were disputed and required adjudication" is an explanation. The second tells you what the estate bought
Does the time relate to the outcome?
Significant time on an asset that realised very little should carry a reason. Sometimes there is a good one, such as a claim that had to be pursued to establish it was worthless
Are the difficulties named?
Cases go wrong. A report that describes only progress, in a case that has plainly stalled, is not telling you the useful part
Does it compare to the estimate?
A narrative that never mentions the original estimate is avoiding the question a creditor most wants answered
Can you tell what happens next, and when?
A report that ends without a next step or a timescale has not finished its job

None of that means an office holder who writes a thin report is doing a poor job. It means you cannot tell from the document, which is precisely what the standard exists to prevent.

If the report does not answer your question

You are entitled to ask. A creditor can request further information about the office holder's remuneration and expenses, and about the work behind them. In Scotland the professional standard is that the office holder considers the request on its merits and answers fairly, in proportion to the case, rather than within a fixed statutory period. In a company voluntary arrangement charged on time there is a harder right: a written statement of the hours spent and the rates charged must be provided free of charge within a set period.

Beyond that, where the remuneration and outlays have been fixed by the committee or the creditors, creditors representing at least a quarter of the debt by value can apply to the court to have them reduced as excessive. That is a serious step and rarely the first one, but the availability of it is what gives the earlier request its weight. Where you believe the process itself has been misused rather than merely poorly reported, complain to the office holder first, and if their answer does not satisfy you the complaint route runs through the Insolvency Service's complaints gateway, which passes it to the office holder's professional body.

In our experience most unsatisfactory reports are the product of a busy case rather than an evasive office holder, and a direct question resolves them. If it does not, contact us and we will tell you whether what you are being told is reasonable.

Our own fees are a different thing

Worth separating, because creditors and directors both conflate the two.

Where Lancefield advises a company on restructuring or an individual on their own position, our fee is agreed with that client directly, before the work starts, and it is paid by them. It is not office-holder remuneration, it does not come out of an insolvent estate, and it is not subject to approval by creditors, because we are not an office holder and there is no estate involved.

If you are a creditor looking at a report and wondering which fees are which: the office holder's remuneration is the amount determined by the committee, or where there is none by the creditors or the court, and drawn from the estate. Anything we were paid for advising the company before the appointment is a pre-appointment cost like any other professional fee, and the office holder will treat it as such.
