Commercial mortgages

A commercial mortgage is long-term borrowing secured on business premises, either property you trade from yourself or property you hold as an investment. Development schemes are funded differently, and our property finance page covers those. It is the most straightforward form of commercial lending and usually the cheapest, because the lender's security is tangible and its position is strong.

For most owner-managed businesses it is also the largest single financial commitment they will make, and the one whose terms get the least scrutiny, because the headline rate is what everyone reads and the covenants are what actually bite.

What lenders actually look at

Three things, in this order. The property, because that is the security: what it is worth, how specialised it is, and how easily it could be sold to someone else. The income, meaning whether the business or the rent covers the payments with room to spare. And the covenants, which are the conditions you agree to keep to, typically around loan-to-value and interest cover. The covenants are where owner-managed businesses get caught, because a breach can be triggered by a valuation moving rather than by anything the business did.

Who it suits, and who it does not

It may suit you if

You are buying premises you will occupy, or refinancing property you already own onto better terms. The income covers the payments with genuine headroom rather than exactly. You expect to hold the property for a period long enough to justify the arrangement costs.

It is the wrong move if

You need the money quickly, in which case this is the wrong instrument and bridging finance is what people use, expensively. Or the business is under strain and the mortgage is being drawn to fund trading, which turns a property asset into a monthly obligation the trade already cannot support.

Enforcement in Scotland works differently

Almost everything written online about commercial mortgage enforcement describes England and Wales, where a lender appoints a receiver under the Law of Property Act 1925. That regime does not exist in Scotland. Security over Scottish property is a standard security, and a lender enforces it under the Conveyancing and Feudal Reform (Scotland) Act 1970 by serving a calling-up notice or a notice of default, then exercising its remedies. Where any part of the property is residential, including a flat above a shop, the lender needs a warrant from the sheriff before it can sell or take possession. If your property is in Scotland, the LPA guidance you will find is describing another country's law, and our LPA receivership page sets out the contrast properly.
