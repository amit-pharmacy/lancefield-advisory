Finance & Funding

Sports and entertainment finance

This part of our finance and funding section covers two related things: funding for clubs, venues and promoters, and funding for individuals in sport and entertainment whose income is large but irregular and whose earning period may be short.

Book a free, confidential call

Both are treated cautiously by mainstream lenders, and for the same underlying reason. The income is real but it is lumpy, uncertain and, in the case of individuals, concentrated into a small number of years.

What the sector funds

For clubs and venues: premises and their improvement, equipment, and working capital across a season whose income arrives in bursts around fixtures, events or a summer. For individuals: property and lifestyle lending underwritten against irregular earnings, which mainstream affordability assessments handle poorly. In both cases the funding challenge is not the total income but its shape.

Who it suits, and who it does not

It may suit you if

There is a demonstrable income pattern over several seasons or years rather than one exceptional period, and the borrowing is sized against a normal year rather than a good one. For a club, there is governance capable of holding to a budget, which lenders in this sector look at closely.

It is the wrong move if

The borrowing is sized against a peak: a promotion, a cup run, a single strong season, a contract that has years left but is not guaranteed. Sport is the sector where optimism most reliably becomes debt, and the commitments made after a good season are the ones that cause the trouble two seasons later.

Why clubs get into difficulty

The pattern is consistent and it is structural rather than a matter of poor management. Costs in sport are competitive: what a club spends is driven by what its rivals spend, because the objective is relative rather than absolute. Income, meanwhile, depends on results, which are uncertain. So costs are committed in advance on the assumption of a level of success that may not arrive, and the gap between the two is filled with debt. Add the fact that a club rarely fails out of public view, because supporters, leagues and sometimes local authorities all have an interest, and you get a sector where insolvency is unusually public and unusually constrained. Where the trade is viable but the historic debt is not, a company voluntary arrangement is the instrument that addresses that, and our company administration page covers the alternative.
