Engineering and manufacturing finance

Engineering and manufacturing businesses have a funding profile that does not fit standard lending well. Capital equipment is expensive and long-lived, tooling for a specific contract is expensive and often worth nothing to anyone else, and there is usually a long gap between winning work and being paid for it.

The result is a sector that is frequently profitable on paper and short of cash in practice, which is a combination that catches out lenders and owners alike.

What the sector actually needs funding for

Three things, and they behave differently. Capital equipment, which has a resale value and is therefore straightforward to fund through asset finance. Tooling and setup for a specific contract, which is much harder, because it is worth little outside that contract and lenders know it. And working capital across the production cycle, from raw material through work in progress to an invoice that then sits on payment terms, which is where most of the strain actually sits.

Who it suits, and who it does not

It may suit you if

You are funding equipment with a genuine second-hand value, or bridging a working capital cycle on contracts you have won and will be paid for. The order book supports the commitment beyond the current contract.

It is the wrong move if

You are funding tooling for a single contract whose margin does not cover it, or borrowing against an order book concentrated in one customer. Both are common and both convert an operational risk into a financial one that outlives the contract.

The pattern we see in engineering failures

Engineering businesses rarely fail because they cannot do the work. They fail because a large contract absorbed working capital faster than it generated cash: materials bought, labour paid, tooling made, and payment arriving months later or in stages that lag the spend. A profitable contract can put a company under, and a growing order book can accelerate it rather than help. That is a capital management question before it is a funding one, and the honest answer is sometimes that the contract should be declined or repriced rather than financed. Where the pattern is already established, our turnaround page is the more useful starting point.
