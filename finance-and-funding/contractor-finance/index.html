Contractor and construction finance

Construction has a payment cycle that would put most other industries out of business. Work is done, an application for payment is made, a proportion is certified, payment arrives on extended terms, and a slice is held back as retention for a year or more after the job is finished.

A contractor can therefore be busy, profitable and insolvent at the same time, and construction records more insolvencies than any other sector, which reflects exactly that. Construction is one of the sectors we work in most.

Where the money is tied up

In three places. In work in progress, done but not yet certified. In certified applications waiting out the payment terms. And in retentions, which are the industry's most peculiar feature: money you have earned, held by someone else, sometimes for years, and frequently difficult to recover at the end. Add plant and vehicles, which are funded through asset finance, and the working capital demand on a growing contractor is substantial and constant.

Who it suits, and who it does not

It may suit you if

You have certified applications or invoices and the funding bridges the payment terms. The main contractors you work for are financially sound. Growth is funded rather than absorbed, which is the distinction that decides whether a busy year helps or hurts.

It is the wrong move if

You are funding a cash gap caused by disputed work or unrecovered retentions, which is a commercial and contractual problem that money does not fix. Or your exposure is concentrated in one main contractor, in which case the funding does not reduce the risk, it increases what you lose if they fail.

When the main contractor goes under

This is the risk that defines the sector, and it runs downhill fast. When a main contractor fails, subcontractors rank as ordinary unsecured creditors for everything certified and unpaid, for work in progress, and for retentions, and unsecured creditors in an insolvency typically recover very little. The knock-on then takes out subcontractors who were themselves perfectly viable. Practical protection is limited but real: know who you are working for and check them, keep applications and certifications current rather than letting them run, resist the temptation to let one relationship dominate your order book, and take retention recovery seriously rather than treating it as an afterthought. If a main contractor above you has failed, act quickly, because our winding up petition and creditors' voluntary liquidation pages describe timetables that move faster than most subcontractors expect.
