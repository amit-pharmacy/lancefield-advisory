Forensic accounting and forensic services

Forensic accounting is the application of accountancy and investigation to questions that will be decided by a court, a tribunal, an arbitrator or a negotiated settlement. It covers three things: finding out what happened to the money, quantifying what someone has lost, and presenting both in a form that survives challenge from the other side.

What we are instructed to do

Fraud and financial crime investigation

A fraud investigation establishes whether money has been taken, how, by whom and how much. Employee theft, false invoicing, procurement fraud, bribery and corruption, misappropriation by a director or partner, and the concealment that usually accompanies financial crime. The output is evidence for a disciplinary, a civil claim, an insurance claim or a police report, and it has to be gathered in a way that keeps all of those options open.

Expert witness and quantum

Quantifying loss in a dispute and giving evidence on it. Loss of profits from a breach of contract, business interruption, the value of what was destroyed or withheld. Reports prepared to the standards courts require, with the reasoning exposed so it can be tested rather than asserted.

Valuation in dispute

Where the value of a company or a shareholding is contested: a shareholder dispute, a divorce, an unfair prejudice petition, or a buyout where the parties cannot agree the number. Different from the valuation work on our corporate finance page, because the audience is a judge rather than a buyer.

Professional negligence

Assessing whether financial advice or accountancy work fell below the standard required, and what that professional negligence cost the client. Instructed by claimants and defendants alike, and the work has to be equally rigorous either way.

Insurance claims

Preparing or reviewing insurance claims where the loss is financial: business interruption, fidelity and crime cover, and disputes about the measure of indemnity. Insurers commission work on insurance claims as often as policyholders do.

Asset tracing and recovery

Following funds and identifying where value has gone. Frequently instructed alongside an administration or a liquidation, where money has left a company before it failed, and often the point at which our forensic and insolvency work meet.

The expert's duty, and why it decides whether the report is useful

This is the part that separates evidence from advocacy, and the sales-led pages on this subject leave it out.

The duty an expert owes runs to the court, and it outranks any duty to whoever is paying. In practice that means independence, a report kept within the limits of the expert's own expertise, the facts and assumptions set out where they can be seen, and, critically, an acknowledgement of anything that qualifies the opinion or points the other way. An expert who omits the inconvenient point is not helping the client; they are creating an opening for the other side and putting their whole report at risk.

So the honest version of what a good forensic accountant offers a solicitor is this: an opinion that is genuinely defensible, reached without being told what it should be, and delivered with the weaknesses identified early enough to be dealt with. That is what an instructing solicitor is buying, and it is not the same as agreement. Where our analysis does not support the client's case, we say so at the outset, when it is still useful, rather than discovering it in the witness box.

In Scotland that duty comes from the case law rather than a rule of court. The Supreme Court in Kennedy v Cordia (Services) LLP adopted the Ikarian Reefer duties for Scottish civil cases, and went further than the English rules do: it treated independence and impartiality as a question of admissibility rather than of weight. A Scottish court can refuse to hear a report that does not meet them.

For that reason we distinguish clearly between advisory work, where we assist a party, and expert work, where we owe the duty to the court. Both are legitimate. Confusing them is what gets reports excluded.

How a forensic investigation runs

  1. Scope it properly.The single biggest cause of wasted forensic cost is an open-ended instruction. What question is being answered, for what forum, by when, and what is the proportionate depth given what is at stake
  2. Secure the evidence.Accountancy records, bank statements, the accounting system's audit trail, emails, contracts. Preservation matters from day one, particularly where an employee is still in post and has access
  3. Establish the baseline.What the records show, and how reliable they are. In fraud work, the pattern of what is missing frequently carries more information than what is present
  4. Analyse.Reconstructing transactions, tracing funds through accounts, testing the numbers against the documents and the documents against each other. Where the matter involves bribery or corruption, the payment trail and the approval trail are examined separately
  5. Quantify.Building the loss calculation on stated assumptions, with sensitivities, so that a change in one assumption does not collapse the whole figure
  6. Report.In the form the forum requires, with the reasoning visible
  7. Support through to conclusion.Meetings of experts, responding to the other side's report, and giving evidence if it goes that far

Matters frequently settle. A well-constructed report is often what causes them to, because it converts an argument into a number both sides can see the basis of, which is why forensic accounting is as much a dispute resolution tool as a litigation one.

In the Scottish courts

Most of the forensic accounting content online describes English procedure, and the practical differences matter if your case is in Scotland.

Proceedings run in the Court of Session or the sheriff court, with a Commercial Court in the Court of Session and a commercial procedure in some sheriff courts for disputes of a commercial or business nature. The vocabulary is different: pursuer and defender rather than claimant and defendant, a proof rather than a trial, and documents lodged as productions. The evidence is that of a skilled witness, which the Rules of Court call a report of a skilled person, though everyone says expert in practice. Recovery of documents runs through commission and diligence rather than the English disclosure regime, and the statutory power is available before proceedings are even raised, which is worth knowing early. The timetable in a commercial action is actively managed by the judge.

None of that changes the accounting. It changes how the work is packaged, when it has to be ready, and who it is addressed to. We are Glasgow-based and this is the system we work in.

Where forensic work meets insolvency

This is our unusual angle, and it comes from the practice rather than the marketing.

A large share of financial wrongdoing surfaces when a company fails, because that is when someone independent looks properly at the records for the first time. Our insolvency practitioner page explains who does that looking and why they are obliged to. Money paid to connected parties, assets transferred before the collapse, a director's loan account that grew without explanation, or trading that continued long after it should have stopped. Where a government body such as the Insolvency Service is already investigating, an independent analysis is worth having early. In Scotland the relevant challenges are gratuitous alienations and unfair preferences, and the equivalent claims elsewhere are transactions at an undervalue and preferences.

We work on both sides of that: instructed by an insolvency practitioner to run the forensic investigation and quantify what was lost, and instructed by directors who need an independent analysis of transactions being questioned. Because we take no insolvency appointments ourselves, we can act in either direction without a conflict, which a firm that also takes appointments frequently cannot. Our insolvency practitioner and liquidation pages cover the procedures that generate this work.

What it costs, and keeping it proportionate

A forensic investigation is charged on time, because its scope is genuinely unknowable at the outset. What is controllable is the shape of it: a defined first phase with a decision point at the end, rather than an open instruction.

We will tell you early where the cost of proving something is likely to exceed what proving it is worth. That happens more often than the industry admits, particularly in smaller frauds where the money has already been spent and the perpetrator has nothing to recover from. A short scoping exercise that concludes the claim is not worth pursuing has saved the client a great deal, and we would rather deliver that answer than bill for the alternative.
