Commercial arbitration and expert determination

Most commercial disputes are an argument about money wearing a legal costume. The contract settles who is entitled to what, and the fight is about the number. Commercial arbitration decides that argument privately, in front of arbitrators the parties choose themselves, and ends in an award that binds both sides.

Arbitration, expert determination, mediation or court

These four are not interchangeable, and choosing wrong is expensive. The useful question is whether your disagreement is about the law or about a number.

Litigation.
Public and adversarial, decided by a judge in the Court of Session or the sheriff court, on an open record your customers and bank can read
Commercial arbitration.
Private, governed by the arbitration agreement in your contract and decided by arbitrators appointed for their expertise. The arbitral award is final and enforceable, and the grounds of appeal are narrow
Expert determination.
One expert decides the point using their own knowledge, usually on paper and in weeks not months. No hearing, no arbitral tribunal, very little room to challenge. Share valuation and earn-out clauses often name it in advance
Mediation.
Nobody decides anything. A mediator helps the parties reach their own settlement, and it earns its place while the relationship still has value

Where the disagreement is really about valuation or quantum, expert determination is often quicker and cheaper than arbitration. Where it turns on what the contract means, you want a lawyer deciding it. Settling that distinction when the clause is drafted saves more than any other choice in dispute resolution.

Scotland has its own arbitration regime

Almost everything written about commercial arbitration in the UK describes the English position. Arbitration seated in Scotland runs under the Arbitration (Scotland) Act 2010, not the Arbitration Act 1996 that applies in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The 2010 Act carries the Scottish Arbitration Rules, some mandatory, some applying only until the parties agree otherwise.

The seat decides which regime governs you, and a seat is a legal address rather than a venue. If the dispute resolution clause in your contract names London, the arbitration is seated in England: the Arbitration Act 1996 governs the procedure and the English court supervises it, usually the Commercial Court, even where every hearing is in Glasgow and the contract itself is governed by Scots law. Scottish businesses sign up to a London seat more often than they realise, because the template came from London.

Two features of the Scottish Arbitration Rules are worth knowing. Confidentiality is written in as a default, where in England it rests on case law. So is the right to appeal to the court on a point of legal error, which the parties can contract out of.

The financial questions these disputes turn on

Valuation disagreements

What a company, a shareholding or a division is worth when the parties cannot agree. Maintainable earnings, minority discounts, directors' loan accounts, and the gap between what a formula produces and what the business would fetch. On a sale rather than a dispute, corporate finance is the page you want.

Shareholder and partnership fallings-out

Exit provisions, the buyout price, disputed drawings, and whether the accounts the price is calculated from can be relied on at all. These start as valuation arguments and become forensic ones. Owners planning an orderly separation should start at exit planning.

Contract and loss claims

Loss of profits from a breach, disputed variations, unpaid milestones, and the counterfactual: what would have happened had the contract been performed. That counterfactual is where claims of this kind are won and lost.

Completion accounts and earn-outs

Arguments after a deal about working capital, accounting policy and whether an earn-out target was met. The share purchase agreement usually sends these to expert determination, and that clause sets what the expert may consider. Our transaction support page covers the deal side.

International arbitration and enforcement

International arbitration exists mainly because of enforcement. A court judgment is hard to enforce against assets held abroad, while an arbitral award travels: the New York Convention obliges the courts of the states signed up to it to recognise an arbitral award made in another signatory state. That treaty is why cross-border contracts choose arbitration at all.

International commercial arbitration usually runs under the rules of an arbitral institution such as the LCIA in London, with ad hoc arbitration under the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules as the alternative. Whichever the arbitration agreement names, the financial evidence is built the same way, although currency, tax and the applicable law can move the figure more than the facts do.
