Transaction support and due diligence

Due diligence is the investigation a buyer runs before committing money, and the preparation a seller does before letting anyone look. It answers one question in detail: is this company what it appears to be? The price adjustments, the warranties and the money held back at completion all follow from what that investigation finds. Larger firms sell the same work as transaction services.

What due diligence actually is

An audit tests whether a set of accounts is fairly stated. Due diligence asks something else: what would a new owner of this company inherit? It is an investigation rather than a compliance exercise, and it produces findings rather than an opinion. Whether the transaction is a merger, a trade purchase or a management buyout, the question is the same.

Scope is agreed at the start and deliberately narrow. Nobody examines everything. A proportionate financial due diligence review concentrates on the few places where the value could be materially wrong, and says what was left alone.

Buy-side: is the company what it appears to be?

You are making an investment decision on evidence that is entirely historic. Buy-side work takes the numbers a seller has presented and establishes which of them survive into the first Monday after completion. Most of the financial risk sits in the first four items.

Quality of earnings.
Reported profit restated as sustainable trading profit: one-off gains out, the owner's pay at a market rate, personal costs removed, policy changes unwound. The normalised EBITDA this review produces is what the multiple applies to
Revenue quality.
Where income comes from, how much is contracted or repeating, customer concentration, and whether any was recognised early
Working capital.
How much cash the business needs at its normal level of trade. Understate it and the buyer funds the gap weeks after completion
Debt-like items.
Unpaid tax and time to pay arrangements, overdue creditors used as finance, deferred consideration from an earlier deal, dilapidations, pension deficits, accrued holiday
Tax exposures.
Historic PAYE and VAT treatment, the employment status of contractors, open HMRC enquiries, and anything a later inspection could unpick
Forecast credibility.
Whether the assumptions trace back to something that has already happened, and whether the synergies behind the offer belong to the target or to the buyer
Operational reality.
Systems, capacity and the few people the trading depends on, which is where the operational cost of year one hides

Two findings recur above all others on owner-managed deals: working capital understated, and profit that depends on an owner working for less than the job is worth. Neither is dishonest. Both move the number.

Vendor due diligence: examined before the buyer arrives

Vendor due diligence is the same investigation run on your own company, commissioned by the seller before the market sees anything. The point is that the buyer's adviser finds nothing that stalls the deal.

What kills owner-managed deals is rarely the price. It is delay and surprise. A buyer who finds in week six what the seller could have explained in week one does not simply adjust the offer. Every unexplained item is then treated as a financial risk.

Awkward things get fixed while there is time.
Related-party balances, a property nobody wants, dormant subsidiaries, an unresolved employment matter. corporate simplification handles the group-structure half
The numbers get told once.
A normalised earnings position the seller can defend, with the adjustments already made and evidenced
Competitive tension survives.
With more than one bidder, late information is what lets a buyer reopen terms after the others have gone home
The owner keeps running the business.
Deals fail because trading dips while the seller answers data requests

Vendor work belongs with exit planning. Treat it as an investment in the sale rather than a cost, and start it well before the memorandum goes out.

Financial, legal, commercial and tax: who does which

Due diligence is spoken about as one job. It is at least four, run by different people at once, and knowing which is which stops you paying twice.

Financial due diligence.
Earnings, working capital, debt-like items, cash and tax exposure. Done by accountants, and it is our part
Legal.
Title to the shares, contracts, property, employment, litigation, and the change of control clauses a new owner triggers by buying the company. Done by solicitors, and in Scotland by a Scottish firm: the title system and the rules for ending a commercial lease are not the English ones
Commercial due diligence.
The market, the customers, the competition, and whether the growth in the forecast is available to anybody at all. Usually a specialist firm, and the workstream most often skipped
Tax.
Historic exposure, plus the structure of the deal. Run alongside the financial work, because each keeps finding the other's problems
Operational and IT.
Systems, capacity, key people, and the operational investment a buyer faces in year one. On smaller deals it folds into the commercial and financial scope
ESG due diligence.
Environmental, social and governance exposure. Standard where private equity or institutional investors sit behind the deal. On a small acquisition, ESG due diligence is a section rather than a workstream

Somebody must own the whole list, because the gaps between workstreams are where things get missed. On a merger that is the corporate finance lead, and our corporate finance page covers the role.

How a due diligence review runs

  1. Scoping.What the review is for, what it will test, what it will not, what it costs and on what terms. In writing, before anything starts
  2. Information request.One specific list, sent early. The pace of the whole review is set here
  3. Analysis.The numbers rebuilt from the underlying records rather than lifted from the accounts, and management answering questions in a session rather than by email
  4. Interim findings.Anything that could change the price or stop the deal is raised the day it is found. A review that saves bad news for the final report has failed
  5. Report.Findings quantified where they can be, each with a recommendation attached: adjust, warrant, indemnify, hold back, or reconsider
  6. Support to completion.Findings have to survive the drafting, so we stay involved while the deal is active

What the findings do to the deal

Findings are worth only what is done with them. A report that sits in a folder while the transaction completes on the terms originally offered has cost money for nothing. Six things a finding can become, and choosing between them is where the negotiation happens.

A price adjustment.
A finding that changes sustainable earnings is multiplied by whatever multiple the deal was struck on, so a modest error in profit becomes a large one in the consideration. In a merger it moves the share exchange ratio instead
A completion mechanism.
How the consideration is trued up afterwards: completion accounts measuring cash, debt and working capital on the day, or a locked box fixing the balance sheet at an earlier date
A warranty.
A statement by the seller that something is true, with a claim if it is not. Warranties cover the unknown, and taking one instead of an adjustment for a problem already found is a poor trade for a buyer
An indemnity.
A pound for pound promise covering one identified exposure, usually a tax one. For the problems the investigation found but could not size
A retention.
Part of the consideration held back against claims, sometimes for years. The only protection that does not depend on finding the seller afterwards
A condition, or no deal.
Some findings cannot be priced. A key contract with a change of control clause and a customer who will not confirm they are staying is a reason to pause

Disclosure runs alongside it. The seller discloses against the warranties, and anything properly disclosed generally cannot then be claimed for. Good transaction services work stays with the commercial terms through the drafting rather than stopping at the report.

When due diligence is not worth doing

We are paid for the work rather than for the deal, which is what lets this section exist.

When the cost is out of proportion to the investment at risk.
On a small asset purchase with no history, no employees and no liabilities coming across, the financial risk is small and a full review buys comfort rather than information
When value sits in assets rather than earnings.
If it is property or plant, a valuation and a solicitor matter more than an earnings review
When you already know.
A management buyout team that has run the company for a decade does not need a stranger to explain its own working capital cycle
When nobody has written the scope.
Open-ended instructions produce long reports and large fees. Our recommendation is usually to narrow the terms of the work to the value genuinely at risk
When the company needs fixing rather than buying.
A distressed target is a different exercise, and an independent business review answers the question better

On a smaller transaction the right scope is a short review of earnings, cash and tax, delivered in days rather than weeks. And if the seller will not release the information, that is the finding.

Where we fit
