Corporate finance

Corporate finance is the advice around the transactions that change who owns a business, what it is worth and how it is funded: mergers and acquisitions, selling a company, buying one, valuing shares, raising capital, or reshaping the capital structure so that the people running the business are the people who own it. For most owners it is the largest financial event of their working life, and it happens once.

What we advise on

Selling a business

Preparing the company for sale, identifying and approaching buyers, running a competitive process where the market supports one, and negotiating the deal through to completion. The preparation is where most of the value is won, which is why it belongs alongside exit planning rather than starting when the business goes to market.

Mergers and acquisitions

Buying a company or a trade, or merging two: assessing whether the target is worth what is being asked, structuring the transaction and its funding, and managing the process through to completion. Most acquisitions by owner-managed businesses are trade purchases rather than mergers, but the discipline is the same. The financial due diligence itself sits on our transaction support page.

Management buyouts and buy-ins

Structuring a purchase by the management team, including how much of the price the business itself can fund out of future profits, what the funders will require, and how the equity is split between the team. Frequently the best outcome where a trade sale would break up something worth keeping.

Valuations

What a business or a shareholding is actually worth, and on what basis. For a transaction, a share transfer, a shareholder agreement, tax purposes, or simply so an owner knows. Where the value is disputed rather than negotiated, that is forensic work and our forensic services page covers it.

Capital raising

Working out what capital structure the business needs and what it can support: debt, equity, or a mix, and on what terms. Capital investment from a private equity house comes with governance conditions as well as money, and those terms deserve as much attention as the valuation. We advise on the structure and introduce funders. We are not brokers and we do not arrange the facility, which our finance and funding page explains.

Shareholder reorganisations

Buying out a departing shareholder, bringing in a new one, resolving a deadlock, or reshaping a group so the ownership matches the intention. These transactions rarely make headlines and frequently do more for shareholder value than a sale would. Often the quiet transaction that prevents a dispute later.

What a business is worth, and why the number moves

Most private companies are valued on a multiple of sustainable earnings, adjusted to show what a new owner would actually inherit: the owner's remuneration replaced at market rate, personal costs removed, and one-off items stripped out. The multiple then depends on things a buyer can verify rather than things a seller asserts.

Scale.
Larger companies attract higher multiples for the same profit, because they carry less risk and reach more buyers, including private equity firms that will not look below a certain size
Recurring revenue.
Contracted or repeat income is worth substantially more than the same profit earned job by job
Customer concentration.
One customer at a large share of revenue is the most common single reason a multiple is marked down
Owner dependence.
If the business needs you, the buyer is acquiring a problem alongside the profit
Growth and market position.
Demonstrated, not projected. Buyers discount forecasts heavily and pay for track record
Quality of information.
Reliable monthly accounts and a clean audit trail reduce perceived risk, and perceived risk is what the multiple prices

Two companies with identical profits routinely sell for very different amounts. The gap is not luck. It is the preparation, which is why the conversation is worth having years before the transaction rather than at the point of sale.

Structure: the difference between the price and the money

This is the part owners understand least and regret most, and it is more important than the headline number.

Cash at completion.
The only part that is certain. Every other element depends on something happening afterwards
Deferred consideration.
A fixed amount paid later. Its value depends entirely on the buyer still being able to pay it, so who the buyer is matters as much as what they promise
Earn-out.
A payment contingent on the business hitting targets after the sale, when you may no longer control the decisions that determine whether it does. Earn-outs are where sellers most often lose money they had counted as theirs. If you accept one, the mechanics of how performance is measured and what the buyer may not do to the business matter more than the headline figure
Shares in the buyer.
Value that depends on somebody else's company, usually with restrictions on when you can sell
Warranties and indemnities.
Promises about the business that, if wrong, let the buyer claim money back. The cap, the time limits and the disclosure exercise decide your real exposure after the deal
Retentions and escrow.
Money held back against warranty claims, sometimes for years

A headline price with half of it deferred, conditional and warranty-exposed is not the same offer as a lower price paid in cash. Comparing offers properly means valuing the certainty, not just the total, and it is the single most useful thing an adviser does.

How a deal actually runs

  1. Preparation.Financial information assembled and normalised, a financial forecast a buyer will believe, the equity story written, and the obvious problems fixed before a buyer finds them
  2. Approach.A targeted list of credible buyers rather than the widest possible net. Confidentiality agreements before anything meaningful is shared
  3. Initial offers.Indicative and non-binding, usually subject to due diligence. This is the point at which a seller has the most negotiating power, and it is where structure should be agreed rather than left
  4. Heads of terms.The commercial deal in outline, including exclusivity. Once exclusivity is granted, the balance of power shifts decisively to the buyer, so what is agreed here matters more than its non-binding status suggests
  5. Due diligence.The buyer's advisers examine everything. Surprises here cost price or kill deals, which is why preparation earns its keep
  6. Documentation.Share purchase agreement, disclosure letter, ancillary documents. Solicitors' work, with the adviser keeping the commercial deal intact through the drafting
  7. Completion, and afterwards.Funds flow, and any post-completion accounts, earn-out periods and warranty periods begin

Three to nine months from decision to completion is typical for an owner-managed business, and the timetable is usually set by due diligence and by how ready the information was.

When not to do a deal

We are paid for advice rather than by completion, so this section can exist.

When the business is not ready
and a year of preparation would change the price by more than the delay costs
When the market is wrong for that business
and waiting is genuinely better than accepting a discount
When the structure transfers the risk to you.
A high headline price mostly in earn-out, from a buyer whose ability to pay depends on the business continuing to perform without you
When the buyer cannot fund it.
Private equity buyers and trade acquirers alike need their capital confirmed, and establishing this early avoids months of due diligence on a transaction that was never going to complete
When there is no buyer at any acceptable price.
For some owner-managed businesses the honest answer is an orderly solvent closure through an MVL, and that is a legitimate outcome rather than a failure
When the business needs fixing rather than selling.
A struggling company sold in distress realises a fraction of its value, and a turnaround or an independent business review is the right route first

What we bring that a pure corporate finance house does not is the other end of the story. We spend the rest of our time on businesses in difficulty, including businesses that got into difficulty because of how a deal was structured: an earn-out that failed, an acquisition funded with debt the business could not service, warranties that turned into claims. That experience shapes how we advise on structure, and it is why our advice tends to be more conservative about deferred consideration than a fee-on-completion adviser's would be.
