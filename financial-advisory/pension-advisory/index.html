Pension advisory

This page is about pensions as a company problem: what an employer still owes when cash is short, what a scheme does to a restructuring or a sale, and where it ranks when a business fails. Lancefield is not authorised by the Financial Conduct Authority, and we give no personal pension advice, no investment advice and no retirement planning advice to individuals.

Auto-enrolment when the money is tight

Automatic enrolment duties under the Pensions Act 2008 do not pause because a business is struggling. The company still enrols eligible staff, still owes the employer contributions it promised, and still has to hand over the employee contributions it deducted from wages.

The deducted money is where directors get into real trouble. It is not the company's money to use, even for a fortnight. It has to reach the scheme by the statutory deadline in the following month, and a trustee who does not receive it has to report the failure to the Pensions Regulator once it looks materially significant, so the Regulator usually hears about arrears without being told.

Enforcement escalates.
A compliance notice, then a fixed penalty, then penalties accruing daily until it is put right
Inducing staff to opt out is prohibited.
Persuading a worker to leave so the employer saves money is its own breach
It follows the director.
Money deducted from wages and not passed on turns up in a conduct report

When the pension payment is the one that gets skipped, the problem is not the scheme, and an independent business review will say how fast the company can catch up.

Defined benefit schemes and the real size of the deficit

A legacy final salary scheme is not a trade creditor. The figure in the accounts is an accounting measure. The figure that decides a restructuring, a sale or an insolvency is what it would cost to buy the members' benefits from an insurer, and it is usually much bigger. That becomes a statutory debt on the employer when the scheme winds up or the employer fails.

The trustees are an organised creditor.
They have their own actuary and covenant adviser, and they negotiate with employers for a living
Money leaving the company gets examined.
Dividends and intra-group payments made while a scheme is in deficit invite questions years later
The Pension Protection Fund sits behind the scheme.
If the employer fails, the scheme may enter PPF assessment and members may end up with less than they were promised at retirement

So a company that looks saleable on its trading numbers can turn out not to be, and the scheme belongs in the restructuring conversation from the first week rather than the last.

The Pensions Regulator, transactions and sales

The Pensions Regulator's powers reach past the company that employs the members. Where a transaction leaves a defined benefit scheme worse off, it can require another group company, or a connected person, to make good the loss. There are also criminal offences aimed at conduct that avoids an employer debt or risks accrued benefits.

That changes how a deal is run rather than whether it can be run. Certain corporate events have to be notified, and clearance can be sought in advance, which binds the regulator only on the facts you actually disclose, but puts the transaction in front of it first. Design a sale or a corporate simplification and involve the scheme afterwards and it goes badly, so the pensions workstream runs alongside the financial due diligence in our transaction support and corporate finance work.

On a sale, staff who move with the business keep most of their employment rights, but occupational pension rights are treated separately and the buyer's obligation is a minimum one. Whether they transfer at all depends on the type of proceedings. In a pre-pack administration the scheme is usually left with the insolvent employer while the trade continues in the buyer's hands. That is lawful, and it is the transaction the regulator examines hardest, so the evidence on price has to exist at the time. Our administration page covers the process.

Where a scheme ranks when a company fails

One scheme produces two claims, and they rank very differently.

Employee money deducted from pay is preferential.
What was taken from wages in the months before the insolvency ranks ahead of floating charge holders and ordinary unsecured creditors, provided the scheme is an occupational one. Unpaid employer contributions are not. Since contracting-out ended they rank with the ordinary unsecured creditors
The scheme's own deficit claim is not.
That statutory debt ranks with the trade creditors, which on a large scheme means the trustees control the creditors' vote
The National Insurance Fund can fill part of the gap.
Certain unpaid amounts are met from the fund on employees' behalf
A qualifying insolvency event starts PPF assessment.
The scheme is then run with a view to whether the Pension Protection Fund takes it on

The ranking is GB-wide. The procedure around it is not: notices go in the Edinburgh Gazette, and a winding up goes to the Court of Session or to the sheriff court where paid-up share capital is £120,000 or less. If you have arrived from a winding up petition, the pension position is one to establish in the first week.
