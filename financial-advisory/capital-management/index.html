Capital management

Capital management is making sure a business has the money it needs, in the form it needs, when it needs it. Working capital management is the short cycle: cash tied up in stock, in work in progress and in customers who have not paid, set against the credit your suppliers give you. Capital structure is the long one: how much of the business is funded by borrowing, how much by its owners, and whether the mix carries the plan.

A profitable company can run out of money

A business wins a bigger order than it has handled before. It buys materials, pays people to do the work, delivers and invoices. Revenue is recorded the day the invoice goes out, so the profit and loss account looks excellent. The customer pays in sixty days. The supplier wants paying in thirty, HMRC wants the VAT on an invoice nobody has settled, and the payroll runs regardless. Growth widens the gap: every order consumes money before it produces any. Overtrading is the old word for it, and it is still a common route into administration for a company with a full order book.

Profit and cash answer different questions. Profit says whether a period of trading was worthwhile. Cash says whether you can meet what falls due this week. The statutory test for insolvency is whether a company can pay its debts as they fall due, whatever the year looked like on paper. Profitability measures performance. Liquidity measures survival.

The cash conversion cycle

This is the number of days between money leaving the business and coming back for the same piece of work, and the most useful single figure in working capital management.

Stock days.
How long goods sit before they sell, your money on the shelf
Work in progress.
Time between doing the work and being entitled to bill for it, often the biggest block of trapped cash
Debtor days.
How long customers take to pay. Not the terms you agreed, the days they take
Creditor days.
How long you take to pay suppliers, the one part of the cycle that funds you
The cycle itself.
Stock plus work in progress plus debtor days, less creditor days: the days you fund yourself

A business running a cycle of seventy days carries seventy days of cost before it is paid. Take a fortnight off that and cash is released permanently, with no lender involved.

Where the cash is trapped

Late invoicing.
Work finished on the fourth, billed at month end, paid sixty days after that
No credit control.
Terms exist on the invoice and nowhere else, and customers pay whoever asks first
Unbilled work in progress.
Jobs part done and part billed, with a milestone nobody agreed at the outset
Equipment bought from the current account.
A ten year asset paid for out of this month's cash, which is what asset finance exists to prevent
VAT and PAYE used as an overdraft.
The most dangerous of them: it works for two quarters and then stops all at once

Some of that is process, fixable within weeks. Some is structural: if your customers pay in ninety days and will not move, chasing changes nothing, and invoice finance may be the answer. We will also say when a facility is only interest on a problem better process would have solved.

How much working capital the business needs

This should be a number rather than a feeling. A week by week cash flow across the next quarter shows the peak requirement rather than the average, and the peak is what matters: a business that averages comfortable and drops below zero for four days in October has a problem in October. An overdraft repayable on demand is not committed money, and liquidity a lender can withdraw in a phone call is not liquidity you can plan around.

Where the numbers need testing from outside, that is what an independent business review is for, and lenders often ask for one.

Does the capital structure support the plan?

The second half of capital management is the shape of the funding itself. A business can be solvent today and funded in a way that will not survive its own plan.

The mix.
How much debt sits against owners' capital. Gearing comfortable at current trading can be uncomfortable at forecast trading
The maturity.
Short term money funding long term assets is the classic error. The overdraft that bought the machine is repayable on demand. The machine is not
The conditions.
Covenants are tested on dates somebody else chose. Knowing which one you would breach first is worth more than knowing your rate
The personal exposure.
Directors' loans, guarantees and security over the family home turn a company decision into a household one

Where the structure has stopped fitting the business, the answer is often debt restructuring: reprofiling repayments, replacing the wrong facility, or agreeing terms with lenders before a covenant test forces it. Where the trading is the problem, turnaround is the route, and contingency planning is for the owner who wants the fallback mapped before needing it.
