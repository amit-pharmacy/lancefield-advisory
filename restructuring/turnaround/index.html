Business turnaround

A turnaround is the work of fixing an underperforming company while it is still a going concern: finding what is actually causing the problem, stabilising the cash position, and changing the things that made the business lose money in the first place. It is the service that sits furthest from insolvency on this site, and the one that most often makes the rest of it unnecessary.

Is your business a turnaround, or something else?

We take no insolvency appointments, so we have nothing to gain from calling your situation worse than it is, or better. Three honest categories:

Too early

The business is profitable but under strain: growing faster than its working capital allows, or carrying a cost base that has drifted. This is ordinary management improvement and often does not need a turnaround specialist at all. What it may need is a hard look at the numbers, which is what an independent business review gives you.

A turnaround

The business is losing money or barely covering its costs, cash is tight, and the causes are structural rather than one-off. There is still a viable business underneath, there are still stakeholders willing to be talked to, and there is enough liquidity to make changes before they run out. This is the page you want.

Past turnaround

The debt is beyond what the business could service even if it performed perfectly, or the market it serves has gone. Change alone will not fix that. What is needed is a company voluntary arrangement to deal with the historic debt, administration to protect and sell what is worth saving, or an orderly liquidation. Spending six months on a turnaround here consumes the value that would have paid creditors.

Most people arrive convinced they are in the second category. A fair number are in the first or third. Establishing which is the first thing worth paying for, and it takes days rather than months.

What actually causes it

After enough of these, the causes repeat. It is almost never the reason the owner gives first.

Margin erosion nobody tracked.
Prices held while costs rose. The business is as busy as ever and makes less on every job. This is the single most common cause we see in owner-managed businesses, and the most fixable
Working capital swallowed by growth.
More sales, more stock, more debtors, and the cash gap widens. A profitable company can run out of money this way and frequently does
A cost base built for a bigger business
than the one that now exists, or for a market that has moved
One customer, one contract or one supplier
carrying too much of the risk. When it goes, there is no cushion
Management information that arrives too late to act on.
Accounts three months in arrears mean every decision is made about a business that no longer exists
Debt taken on for the wrong reason,
or on terms the business could only service in a good year
A problem the owner already knows about and has not addressed
usually because it involves a person

The diagnosis matters because the wrong one is expensive. Cutting costs in a business whose real problem is pricing makes it smaller and no more profitable, and it is the most common wasted year in this work.

The first ninety days

Turnaround work front-loads. What happens in the first three months decides whether there is a business to improve.

Stabilise the cash.
A weekly short-term cash forecast, built properly and updated every week, replacing the monthly management accounts as the instrument you steer by. Almost every business we work with does not have one when we arrive, and every one of them keeps it afterwards
Establish the true position.
What the business actually owes, to whom, and when. What it is actually owed and how much of that will really arrive. Where the liquidity is trapped: stock, debtors, work in progress, an overdrawn account nobody has looked at
Find the profit by product, customer and job.
Not the overall margin. The distribution. There is nearly always a segment losing money that the average conceals, and finding it is often the whole answer
Buy time with the people who can grant it.
HMRC, the bank, the landlord, key suppliers. Approached early with a credible plan, most will work with you. Approached after a default, most will not
Decide what stops.
The loss-making line, the customer who never pays, the site that does not work. These decisions get harder the longer they wait and they are the ones owners most want to defer
Fix the reporting.
Whatever the plan, it needs numbers that arrive within days rather than months. Without that, nobody can tell whether the turnaround is working

Stabilisation is not the same as recovery. It buys the room in which the actual changes can be made, and a plan that stops at stabilisation simply postpones the problem.

Getting the stakeholders to hold

A turnaround is as much a negotiation as an operational exercise, because everyone with a claim on the business can end it while you are still fixing it.

The bank or lender.
Wants to know that someone competent has their hands on it and that the information is reliable. Lenders support turnarounds far more often than directors expect; what they will not tolerate is being surprised. If yours has commissioned an independent business review, that is a signal to engage, not to retreat
HMRC.
Usually the largest creditor in a struggling small company. Time to Pay is available and is granted on a realistic proposal, but arrears that keep building while other creditors are paid become a conduct issue later
Suppliers.
Continuity of supply is often the thing the business cannot survive losing. Key suppliers generally prefer a customer that recovers to one that fails owing them money, and will say so if asked honestly
Employees.
The people who will deliver the changes and the ones who see the wobble first. Uncertainty costs you the staff you can least afford to lose. What you can say may be limited, but silence is read as the worst case
Shareholders and family.
In an owner-managed business these are often the same people, and the emotional weight of the decisions sits with them

The common thread is that early, honest contact preserves support, and late contact destroys it. That is not a moral observation. It is what we watch happen.

The plan, and making it survive

A turnaround plan is a small number of specific changes with owners, dates and a number attached to each. Anything longer than a page or two is a report rather than a plan, and reports do not change businesses.

What separates the plans that work is that someone is held to them weekly, that the financial performance is measured against the forecast rather than against last year, and that the difficult item is still on the agenda in week three. That is where an outside adviser earns their fee: not for knowing what needs doing, which the owner usually does, but for making sure it happens and for saying the thing everyone in the business is avoiding.

Where the numbers show the plan is not working, the plan changes or the route changes. A turnaround that misses its targets month after month is consuming the value that other options would have preserved, and knowing when to stop is part of the discipline.

Trading on while insolvent: the line to watch

This is the part that generic turnaround advice leaves out, and it matters more than any of the commercial work.

If the company is or becomes unable to pay its debts, a director's duties shift towards the interests of creditors. Continuing to trade past the point where insolvent liquidation or administration could no longer realistically be avoided, with the creditors worse off as a result, is wrongful trading, and it can carry personal liability.

That does not mean a turnaround is dangerous. It means a turnaround needs a documented basis: a plan that a reasonable director could believe in, contemporaneous evidence of the advice taken and the reasoning applied, and a review point at which you will stop if it is not working. Directors who do that are in a materially stronger position than those who simply pressed on, and our contingency planning page covers how to build the fallback alongside the plan.

The practical rule: take advice at the point when you first wonder whether you should, not at the point when someone tells you that you must.

When a turnaround needs something formal alongside it

Operational change fixes the future. It does not remove what the company already owes, and where the historic debt is the binding constraint, the commercial work needs a formal instrument beside it.

Debt restructuring
where the borrowing is serviceable on different terms: reprofiled repayments, consolidated facilities, or new money on a realistic basis
A CVA
where the debt is beyond renegotiation but the trading business is sound. It binds the whole unsecured creditor body to a repayment plan and leaves the directors in control
Administration
where the business needs protection from creditors while the change is made or a sale arranged
A pre-pack administration
where the trade is viable but the company is not, and the value would be lost in a public process

Running the commercial turnaround and the formal route in parallel is normal and often the best outcome. What does not work is doing the operational work for a year first and reaching the formal conversation with the value already spent.
