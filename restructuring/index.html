Restructuring

Business restructuring

Restructuring is what a business does while it still has choices. It covers the changes that fix the underlying position: reshaping debt, improving cash flow, selling or closing the part that is losing money, and getting an honest read on whether the business works once those things are done.

The question underneath everything

Broadly there are two kinds of work here. Financial restructuring changes what the company owes and how it is funded. Operational restructuring changes how the business runs: what it sells, at what margin, and at what cost. Most companies that come to us need some of both, and treating a financial restructuring as sufficient when the operational problem is untouched is the most common way this work fails.

Every restructuring decision turns on one thing: is there a viable business under the current problem?

If there is, then the debt, the structure and the cost base are all solvable, and the work is worth doing. If there is not, then restructuring delays an outcome rather than changing it, and the honest answer is to say so early rather than spend eighteen months and a lot of money arriving at the same place.

Answering that question properly is why an independent business review exists. It gives you, and any lender who is asking, an objective read on whether the business model works once the immediate pressure is removed. It is often the first piece of work we do, because everything else depends on the answer.

What restructuring covers

Turnaround

Addressing the cause rather than the symptom. Operational and financial change in a business that is underperforming but fundamentally sound: pricing, cost base, working capital, management information, and the discipline to act on it.

Debt restructuring

Reshaping what the company owes so that repayment matches what the business can actually generate. Renegotiating terms, consolidating facilities, dealing with a lender's concerns before they become a default.

Independent business review

An objective assessment of viability, usually commissioned by a lender but valuable to a director in its own right. It establishes the facts everyone then negotiates around.

Contingency planning

Preparing a fallback before circumstances force the decision. It is the least urgent-sounding service here and frequently the most valuable, because a plan made calmly beats one made under a deadline.

Contingency planning

Exit planning

Getting a business ready to be sold or wound down on your terms, over a horizon long enough to influence the outcome. Sale, succession, or an orderly solvent closure.

Exit planning

Corporate simplification

Removing dormant and redundant companies from a group structure, reducing filing obligations, cost and risk. Usually executed through solvent liquidations, which is where it touches the insolvency side of the practice.

How a restructuring process actually runs

Companies arrive at this expecting something abstract. In practice the restructuring process follows a fairly consistent sequence, and knowing it makes the whole thing less daunting.

  1. Establish the facts.What the company actually owes, to whom, secured or unsecured, and what the cash position looks like week by week rather than month by month. A surprising number of businesses cannot answer this on day one, and nothing useful happens until they can
  2. Test viability.Strip out the financing problem and ask whether the trading business generates cash. This is the independent business review question, and it determines everything that follows
  3. Map the stakeholders.Lenders, HMRC, landlords, key suppliers, shareholders and employees all have different interests and different degrees of power over the outcome. A restructuring that ignores one of them tends to fail at exactly the point that stakeholder notices
  4. Build the plan.What changes, in what order, funded how, over what period. The plan has to be credible to the people being asked to support it, not merely to you
  5. Negotiate.Lenders and creditors are asked to agree new terms. Companies that come with evidence get better outcomes than companies that come with optimism
  6. Implement and monitor.The plan runs, and it is measured against the forecast every month. Corporate restructuring fails more often through poor follow-through than poor design

Most of the value sits in the first two steps. Companies that get an honest answer on viability early either save a great deal of money or stop spending it on something that was never going to work.

The output of all this is a restructuring plan: a written document setting out the financial position, the changes proposed, the funding required and the forecast that results. Lenders and other companies in the chain will judge you on that plan, so it needs to survive scrutiny rather than merely sound encouraging. A restructuring plan built on assets nobody has valued, or on financial projections that assume the last three years were unrepresentative, will not.

Getting the stakeholders onside

Business restructuring is, in the end, a negotiation with people who have money at risk. Each of them behaves differently and each needs handling.

Lenders

A secured lender's first instinct is to protect its position, which usually means it would rather support a credible plan than enforce and recover less. What it will not tolerate is surprises, or forecasts that turn out to be optimistic. Lenders often commission an independent business review before agreeing anything, and it is better to expect that than to resist it.

HMRC

Frequently the largest unsecured creditor in an owner-managed company. HMRC will discuss Time to Pay, and is considerably more receptive before enforcement has started than after. Where tax debt is the core problem, engaging early is the single highest-value action available.

Key suppliers

A supplier that cannot be replaced has more sway than the size of its debt suggests. Identify those companies early. Some will support a restructuring for the sake of the ongoing relationship if they are told what is happening.

Shareholders

Restructuring often changes the shareholding, particularly where new money comes in. Owner-managers should understand what dilution they are accepting before agreeing to funding, not after.

Employees

Rarely consulted, frequently the people who know where the money is going. They also notice long before any announcement, and silence is generally read as worse news than the truth.

Where restructuring meets the formal procedures

There is no hard border between restructuring and insolvency. Several formal procedures are restructuring tools, and choosing one does not mean the business is finished.

A company voluntary arrangement is a restructuring of the ordinary unsecured debt, binding those creditors to a repayment plan while the company keeps trading and the directors stay in control. Secured creditors keep their security and preferential creditors keep their priority unless they agree otherwise. Administration provides a statutory moratorium, holding creditors off while a rescue or a sale is arranged, and a pre-pack administration does that with a buyer already lined up. Each requires a licensed insolvency practitioner to take an appointment, so each is a formal insolvency procedure, but the purpose of all three is preserving something rather than ending it.

On the solvent side, corporate simplification and exit planning use a members' voluntary liquidation to close down companies that have served their purpose, which is restructuring of a very different kind: tidying a group rather than rescuing one.

What decides between an informal restructuring and a formal procedure is usually whether anyone can block it. If creditors will agree to new terms voluntarily, no formal process is needed. If one creditor can block a sensible outcome that everyone else supports, a procedure that binds the minority becomes the only route. Where the dissenting creditors are ordinary unsecured ones, that is the practical case for a CVA. Where the creditor holding things up is secured or preferential, a CVA cannot bind them without their agreement, and a restructuring plan under Part 26A of the Companies Act 2006 is the procedure that can. Either option is worth understanding before it is needed rather than after.

Directors sometimes resist involving an insolvency practitioner at all, on the reasonable-sounding basis that doing so makes the situation official. In practice an insolvency practitioner advising on options has taken no appointment and nothing is formal, and their view on whether administration or a CVA would work is worth having early. The point at which you cannot avoid an insolvency practitioner is the point at which you have lost the ability to choose which one.

When restructuring is the right call

Some patterns come up repeatedly, and they are worth recognising early.

Profitable but out of cash.
A business can be trading well and still be unable to pay its debts as they fall due. That is a working capital and funding problem, not an insolvency one, and it is very fixable
One loss-making part dragging the rest down.
Where a group or a division is the problem, restructuring can isolate it without losing what works
A lender getting nervous.
Covenant pressure or a facility coming up for renewal is a reason to act now, with a plan, rather than wait to be asked
The numbers have stopped being trustworthy.
If management information is poor, nobody, including you, actually knows whether the business works. That is fixable and it is usually first
A change of ownership coming.
Retirement, a departing shareholder, or a sale in mind, all of which go better with two years' preparation than two months'

When it is not

We would rather say this here than after taking a fee.

Restructuring will not save a business whose product or market has gone. It will not fix a company that cannot generate cash at any plausible cost base. And it will not help where the position has already deteriorated so far that creditors have taken control of the timetable, which is what a winding up petition effectively does.

Where that is the case, the options move to the insolvency practitioner side of the practice: a company voluntary arrangement if the business is viable but the debt is not, administration if protection and a sale are needed, or liquidation if the answer is closure. Knowing which side of that line you are on is exactly what the first conversation establishes.

Refinancing, and when new money is the answer

Refinancing is often the cleanest restructuring available. Where a business is fundamentally sound but the debt is the wrong shape, replacing expensive short-term borrowing with facilities that match the trading cycle can solve the problem without changing anything else.

The usual candidates are invoice finance where cash is tied up in the sales ledger, asset finance to release value from equipment, and refinancing property to spread repayment over a sensible horizon. Companies in the same industry frequently use quite different funding structures, and the mix that suits a contractor is not the one that suits a manufacturer.

Two cautions. Refinancing that simply buys time without changing the underlying economics postpones the problem and adds cost to it. And most new facilities will require personal guarantees, so a director should understand exactly what they are signing before improved cash flow makes it feel routine.

We are advisers rather than brokers. Where funding is the answer we will say so and introduce you to people who arrange it, and we will tell you plainly if we think more debt is the wrong medicine.

Selling, buying, and reshaping the group

Not all restructuring is defensive. Mergers and acquisitions are restructuring tools, and for companies in a difficult position a sale of part of the business is frequently the fastest route to stability.

Selling a profitable division to fund the rest is a legitimate and often overlooked option. So is acquiring a competitor's assets when they fail, which is how a good deal of consolidation in any industry actually happens. Both need the same groundwork: clean numbers, a defensible valuation, and enough time to run a proper process rather than a distressed one.

For groups, the work is frequently about efficiency rather than survival. Dormant companies still carry filing obligations, director duties and annual cost, even where they qualify for audit exemption. Removing them reduces cost and risk, and that is what corporate simplification does.

Where a sale cannot be arranged in the ordinary way because time has run out, a pre-pack administration can achieve one immediately. It is a legitimate route, and a sale to a connected party within the first eight weeks needs either creditor approval or an independent evaluator's report, precisely because it moves fast.

Why the timing is a legal question, not only a commercial one

Directors owe their duties to the company, and normally that means to the shareholders. Once a company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or an insolvent liquidation or administration becomes probable, those duties shift and creditors' interests have to be taken into account. The Supreme Court settled the point in 2022 and was clear that a distant risk of trouble is not enough to engage it: the company has to be at or near the line.

That matters here because it is the same line this whole section is about. Carrying on and hoping, once you knew or should have concluded there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding an insolvent liquidation or administration, is what wrongful trading means, and it can be met with an order to contribute personally. Taking advice, documenting what you decided and why, and acting on it is the practical answer, and it is another reason the date on which you pick up the phone matters more than the sophistication of the plan.

Why the timing matters more than the technique

The single biggest determinant of outcome in this work is when someone picks up the phone. Not the sophistication of the plan, not the size of the adviser, not the finance available. The date.

A company with six months of runway has access to funding, can negotiate with creditors from a position of choice, can sell an asset properly rather than quickly, and can plan a restructuring over a sensible horizon. The same company with six weeks of runway has almost none of that, and the same advisers will be recommending very different things.

Directors delay for understandable reasons: it feels like admitting failure, they expect a big invoice, or they think asking for advice starts something formal. None of those is true of an initial conversation, and the cost of waiting is usually far higher than the cost of asking.
