Restructuring

Debt restructuring

Corporate debt restructuring means changing what a company owes, or when and how it repays, so that the financial obligations match what the business can actually generate. It covers everything from reprofiling a single loan to a full renegotiation across several lenders, and it is the right answer where the trading business works but the borrowing on top of it does not. Some of it is balance sheet restructuring; most of it, in an owner-managed company, is simpler than that.

When restructuring is the right answer

We take no insolvency appointments and we are not brokers, so we are not paid by the outcome. The honest test has three parts.

The business is viable on its own terms.
Strip out the debt service and the company makes money, or would within a reasonable period. If it does not, the problem is the trade and a turnaround comes first
The borrowing is serviceable on some realistic profile.
A longer loan term, a lower interest rate, a payment holiday, interest-only for a period, or a smaller principal after a partial repayment. If no realistic profile works, the answer is a CVA, administration or a liquidation, and the question becomes an insolvency one rather than a refinancing one
The lenders have a reason to say yes.
A lender agrees to a restructuring plan when it expects to recover more that way than by enforcing. Your job, and ours, is to show that arithmetic honestly, with a cash flow that supports it

Where all three hold, restructuring is usually achievable and often undramatic. Where the third fails because the security covers the debt comfortably, expect a harder conversation: a lender that is fully covered has less to lose from enforcing.

The options, and what each is actually for

Reprofiling the repayments

Extending the loan term, moving to interest-only for a period, or agreeing a payment holiday. The cheapest and most common form of debt management, and the one most often agreed, because the lender keeps its principal and its security and simply waits longer. It suits a company with a temporary cash flow squeeze and a credible recovery, and it is where most debt restructuring in owner-managed businesses begins and ends.

Consolidation and refinancing

Replacing several facilities with one, or moving to a different lender on a better interest rate. Debt consolidation simplifies the covenants and often reduces the monthly cost, but consolidation does not reduce what is owed. Where refinancing is the route, we introduce you to a finance and funding specialist rather than acting as broker ourselves.

Releasing trapped working capital

Often the fastest improvement available and frequently missed. Invoice finance against the debtor book, asset finance against plant already owned, or a sale and leaseback of property. It changes the shape of the balance sheet rather than the size of the debt, and it can create the room a restructuring needs.

Debt for equity

Debt-for-equity swaps exchange some of what a lender is owed for a shareholding. A debt-for-equity swap removes borrowing permanently and is standard practice in larger restructurings, but it dilutes the owner and is rare in owner-managed businesses because the creditor rarely wants the shares. Worth knowing about; worth being realistic about.

A negotiated write-down

A creditor accepts less than the full amount to settle. It happens, usually where the alternative is demonstrably worse for them, and it needs evidence rather than argument. Informally agreed write-downs bind only the creditors who sign, which is their limitation.

A formal arrangement

Where the unsecured creditor body is too large or too fragmented to negotiate with individually, a CVA binds all of them to a repayment plan once 75% by value approve it and a majority of the unconnected creditors do not vote it down. It is the instrument for exactly the situation informal restructuring cannot reach.

HMRC, and why it is different

In most owner-managed companies HMRC is the largest creditor, and it does not behave like a bank.

Time to Pay is a genuine and widely granted arrangement, but it is a payment plan rather than a restructuring of the debt: HMRC expects the full amount, and expects you to keep filing and paying current taxes, or to come back and ask for a new bill to be added to the plan. A proposal that assumes a write-down of tax is not a proposal HMRC will engage with, and a second Time to Pay arrangement after a first one failed is a much harder conversation than the first.

What causes lasting damage is letting arrears of taxes build while other creditors are paid. Beyond the immediate risk of enforcement, it is exactly the kind of conduct an insolvency practitioner has to report on if the company later fails, and it is treated as a conduct issue rather than a commercial one.

What lenders actually agree to

We sit on both sides of these conversations, and the pattern is consistent.

They will extend time far more readily than they will reduce principal.
Time costs them margin. A write-down costs them capital and has to be justified internally
They want reliable information, presented once.
A short-term cash flow forecast, a realistic set of assumptions, and an explanation of what went wrong that does not blame the weather. Borrowers routinely underestimate how much credibility a well-built cash flow buys
They will commission their own view.
An independent business review is the normal step for anything material, and it is a signal to engage rather than to panic. The review establishes the facts everyone then negotiates around
They want to see the equity contribute.
Where owners are asking a lender to wait, lenders ask what the shareholders are putting in. Sometimes that is money; more often it is deferred remuneration, a director's loan left in place, or a personal guarantee
They will not restructure into a loss.
If enforcing recovers more than waiting, they enforce, and the conversation turns into an insolvency one. Where a lender holds a fixed charge over property, that may mean an LPA receivership in England and Wales or enforcement of a standard security in Scotland
They move faster on a live default than on a warning.
Approach before the covenant breaks, not after

None of that is unreasonable, and understanding it changes how the proposal is written. Most failed restructurings we see failed on presentation rather than on substance.

The restructuring process

The restructuring process is the same whether one lender is involved or five, and it runs in a set order.

  1. Establish the real position.Total borrowing, interest rate and loan term on each facility, security, covenants, personal guarantees. What is secured on what, and in what order. Directors frequently do not have this in one place, and it is where the work starts
  2. Build the forecast.Weekly short-term cash flow, and a longer projection showing what the business can service. It has to survive a lender's scrutiny, so the assumptions matter more than the presentation
  3. Identify what is achievable.Which of the restructuring options above fit the facts, what each would deliver, and which lenders and other stakeholders need to agree to what
  4. Prepare the proposal.One document, consistent across every lender, showing the position, the plan and what each creditor is being asked for. Different stories to different lenders is the fastest way to lose the room
  5. Negotiate.Usually several rounds with the stakeholders in turn. Secured lenders first, since their consent frequently conditions everyone else's
  6. Document it.Amended facility agreements, new security, waivers, standstill agreements. This is solicitors' work and it is not the place to economise
  7. Monitor and report.Restructured facilities come with tighter reporting and covenants. Meeting them is what keeps the arrangement alive

Timescales for the restructuring process run from a few weeks for a single-lender reprofiling to several months where there are multiple secured creditors, pension obligations or a formal arrangement involved.

What it means for you as a director

Two things to be clear-eyed about.

First, personal guarantees normally survive a restructuring unless the lender expressly releases them, and lenders rarely do. If anything, a restructuring is the moment a lender asks for more security rather than less. Establish what you have already guaranteed before you negotiate, because it changes what you should be willing to agree to. Where a guarantee is called, that becomes a personal matter and our Debt Help Scotland section covers the options.

Second, restructuring while insolvent carries the same duties as trading while insolvent. If the company is or becomes unable to pay its debts, your duties shift towards creditors as a whole, and a restructuring that a reasonable director could not have believed in is not a defence. Take advice, document the basis of the plan, and set a review point at which you stop. Our contingency planning page covers building the fallback in parallel, which is what turns a restructuring attempt into a reasoned decision rather than a gamble.

When restructuring is the wrong answer

It is worth saying plainly, because nobody else on this subject does.

Where the borrowing could never be repaid
on any realistic profile. Debt restructuring here buys months and spends the value that would have gone to creditors, and the honest answer is insolvency advice
Where the trade itself has stopped working.
New terms on the borrowing do not fix a business that loses money before debt service
Where the only new money available is expensive short-term borrowing
taken to service existing debt. This is the most common route from a difficult position to an impossible one
Where a single creditor can and will enforce regardless.
If a winding up petition is coming, the timetable is no longer yours and the winding up petition page is the more urgent read

In each of those, the better outcomes come from the formal insolvency routes: a CVA, administration, or an orderly liquidation while there is still something to distribute.
