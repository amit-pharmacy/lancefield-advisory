Optimised exit planning

Exit planning is the work of getting a business ready to leave: deciding which route out fits, then spending the time before the exit making the company worth more to whoever takes it on, or making a closure orderly rather than forced. Every owner exits eventually. The difference between a planned exit and an unplanned one is usually measured in years of preparation and a substantial share of the value.

The uncomfortable part is that most owner-managed businesses are not saleable in the form their owner imagines. Not because they are bad businesses, but because they depend on the owner, or the numbers do not evidence what the owner knows to be true, or there is no obvious buyer for that size of company in that sector. All three are fixable with enough notice. None is fixable in the last six months.

The routes out

There are five, and the honest work is establishing which of them your business actually supports rather than which one you had in mind.

Trade sale

Selling to a competitor, a supplier, a customer or a consolidator. Usually the highest price, because a trade buyer can strip out duplicated cost and buys market share as well as profit. It is also the most intrusive process: full due diligence, warranties, and a buyer who will know everything about the business before completion.

Sale to a financial buyer

Private equity or an investment company. Realistic for businesses above a certain scale and profitability, and usually structured so that some of the price depends on future performance. Owners frequently stay on for a period, which is either the attraction or the problem depending on the owner.

Management buyout

Selling to the management team, often funded partly by the business itself over time. The price is normally lower than a trade sale but the process is faster, more private, and the business continues in familiar hands. It works where there is a management team capable of running it without you, which is the same thing that makes a trade sale easier.

Family succession

Passing the business to the next generation. The financial questions are the easy part; governance, fairness between family members who are in the business and those who are not, and whether the successor actually wants it are the hard ones. Started early it works well. Assumed rather than planned, it is how family businesses fail.

Solvent closure

Where there is no buyer, or where the business is genuinely the owner and does not survive without them, the right answer is to stop while the company is solvent, pay everyone in full, and take the reserves out as capital through an MVL. This is a legitimate and frequently optimal exit, not a failure, and it is the route the rest of the internet does not discuss.

A sixth route exists and is worth naming so nobody drifts into it: running the business until it stops. That is not an exit, and it converts a solvent closure into an insolvent one.

Why the work starts years ahead

The value of a business at exit is set long before the exit, by things that take time to change.

Owner dependence.
If the customer relationships, the technical knowledge and the decisions all sit with you, a buyer is not buying a business, they are buying a job with your name on it. Building a management layer takes two to three years and is the single largest lever on price
The quality of the numbers.
Buyers pay for evidenced profit, not explained profit. Clean management accounts, a defensible normalisation of the owner's remuneration and personal costs, and a couple of years of consistency are worth more than a good story
Customer concentration.
One customer at forty per cent of revenue takes a visible chunk off the multiple, and diversifying takes years
Contracts and recurring revenue.
Repeatable income is valued far above the same profit earned project by project. Where the business can be moved towards contracts, that shift is worth doing before the sale rather than describing during it
The awkward items.
A property in the company that a buyer does not want, a related-party arrangement, dormant subsidiaries, a pension advisory issue, unresolved litigation. Each is a discount or a delay at due diligence, and each takes months to tidy. Corporate simplification deals with the group-structure half of that
Your own position.
What you need from the exit, what you will do afterwards, and how long you are prepared to stay. Owners who have not answered these negotiate badly

Two to three years is the usual horizon for material improvement. A year is enough to tidy and prepare. Under six months, you are selling what you have.

What the business is actually worth

Most owner-managed businesses are valued on a multiple of sustainable earnings, adjusted for what a new owner would really face: your remuneration replaced with a market-rate manager, one-off items stripped out, and any personal costs removed. That adjusted figure is usually lower than the profit an owner has in mind and higher than the one their accounts show, which is why the first honest valuation conversation is often uncomfortable.

The multiple applied depends on things a buyer can see rather than things you can assert: sector, scale, growth, recurring revenue, customer spread, and how much of the business walks out with you. Two companies with the same profit can be worth very different amounts, and the gap between them is exactly what exit planning is for.

The structure matters as much as the headline. Cash at completion, deferred consideration, an earn-out tied to future performance, shares in the buyer, a period of consultancy. A high price mostly payable later, conditional on results you no longer control, is not the same as the number in the press release. Preparing the business

Build the management layer.
Delegate genuinely, including the customer relationships. The test is whether the business runs for a month without you
Fix the reporting.
Monthly management accounts that arrive within a fortnight and reconcile to the year end. Buyers discount uncertainty and this is where uncertainty shows
Normalise the accounts.
Take the personal costs out, document the adjustments, and let a couple of clean years accumulate before you go to market
Sort the balance sheet.
Deal with the director's loan account, the property, the dormant subsidiaries and the old assets. Anything a buyer has to ask about is a negotiating point for them
Secure what you are selling.
Written customer contracts, employment contracts for key staff, registered intellectual property, a lease with enough term left
Prepare for due diligence early.
Assemble the information a buyer's advisers will want before you need it. Deals fall over on delay and surprises far more often than on price

Almost all of this improves the business whether you sell or not, which is why it is worth doing even where the exit is uncertain.

Tax, and the point of getting it right early

How an exit is structured determines how it is taxed, and the decisions that matter are made before a deal is agreed rather than after. Selling shares and selling the trade and assets produce very different outcomes for the seller. Business Asset Disposal Relief has qualifying conditions that must be met for a period before the disposal, so eligibility is something to check years ahead rather than assume at completion. Where the exit is a solvent closure, the distribution comes out as capital through a liquidation and there is a separate set of rules, covered on our MVL page.

We are not tax advisers and this page is not tax advice. What we do is make sure the tax question is asked at the point when the answer can still change, and work alongside your accountant so that the structure and the tax position are decided together rather than sequentially.

When the honest answer is that there is no buyer

A meaningful share of owner-managed businesses cannot be sold at any price the owner would accept. The business is the owner, or the sector has no acquirers at that scale, or the profit does not survive replacing the owner's labour at market rate.

That is not a failure and it is not the end of the planning. It changes the objective: instead of maximising a sale price, you are maximising what the business generates between now and closure, and then closing it while it is solvent so the accumulated reserves come out efficiently. Done deliberately, that is frequently worth more than a distressed sale would have been, and it is entirely within your control.

What makes it go wrong is deciding too late. A business run past the point where it can pay its own way turns a solvent closure into an insolvent one, and the difference between an MVL and a creditors' voluntary liquidation is the whole of the owner's remaining value plus an investigation into their conduct.
