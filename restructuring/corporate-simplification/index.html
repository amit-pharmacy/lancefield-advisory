Corporate simplification

Corporate simplification is the removal of dormant companies, redundant entities and duplicated ones from a group structure. Most organisations accumulate them without deciding to: an acquisition brings three subsidiaries with it, a trading name gets its own company, a joint venture ends but the entity does not, a property is held separately for reasons nobody can now recall. Each one carries filing obligations, professional fees, directors' duties and a small amount of risk, indefinitely, in return for nothing.

What each dormant company actually costs you

Owners consistently underestimate this, because no single item is large.

Compliance costs.
A confirmation statement for every entity, every year, whether or not it does anything, and accounts too unless the entity qualifies as an exempt dormant subsidiary. That exemption is bought by the parent guaranteeing the subsidiary's liabilities, which is rarely free. Then the finance team's time and the accountancy fee on top
Directors' duties.
Every company needs directors, and those directors carry statutory duties to a company they have probably not thought about since 2019. That is a corporate governance exposure as much as an administrative one. Filing defaults attract a civil penalty against the company and a criminal offence against every director in default, and it is the conviction rather than the penalty that lands on the public record against a name
Risk that does not expire.
Old trading liabilities, dilapidations on a lease nobody cancelled, warranties given on a disposal, a guarantee the group forgot it gave. A dormant company is not a closed one, claims can be brought against it, and if one of them cannot be met the entity is heading for a creditors' voluntary liquidation rather than a tidy closure
Audit and consolidation drag.
More entities means more group reporting, more intercompany reconciliation, and more work for the finance team at every year end
Deal friction.
In due diligence on a sale, a buyer's advisers examine every company in the group. Each dormant entity is a set of questions, a warranty and a potential retention. This is where the real cost lands, and it is why the tidy-up belongs in exit planning rather than after it
Trapped value.
Reserves sitting in a subsidiary that could be in the shareholders' hands

Individually, small. Across fifteen entities over ten years, a material and entirely avoidable running cost for the business.

The decision that matters: strike-off or solvent liquidation

There are two ways to close a solvent company, and choosing between them is the whole of this exercise for any entity holding money.

Strike-off

An application to Companies House to dissolve the company. Cheap, simple, and the right answer for a genuinely empty entity. Its limit is tax: a distribution before strike-off is treated as capital only up to £25,000 in total. A pound over and the whole amount is taxed as income, not just the excess. A creditor can also object and stop the application going through.

Members' voluntary liquidation

A formal solvent liquidation conducted by a licensed insolvency practitioner. It costs more and takes longer, and in return the whole distribution is treated as capital however large it is, the company's affairs are formally closed off with a liquidator's final account, and creditors are dealt with properly rather than left to object. Our MVL page covers it in full.

The practical rule across a group: strike off the genuinely empty entities, and put anything holding reserves above the threshold through an MVL. Our liquidation page sets out how the solvent and insolvent routes differ. Getting that wrong on a single subsidiary can cost more than the whole simplification project. Note also that the twelve-month statutory test for solvency applies to each company separately, so each entity is assessed on its own position rather than the group's.

How the work runs

  1. Map the group structure.Every entity, its status, its accounts, what it owns and owes, who its directors are, and whether it is actually dormant or merely quiet. The management team is regularly surprised by what this turns up
  2. Decide what each company is for.Anything with a business purpose stays. Every other entity is a candidate, and the burden is on keeping it rather than closing it
  3. Do the diligence before closing.Intercompany balances, guarantees given, leases, contracts with change-of-control or dissolution clauses, pension participation, VAT group membership, registered intellectual property, and any bank account or asset still in that company's name. This diligence is the whole of the risk, and it is where a rushed project goes wrong
  4. Deal with the assets.Transfer intellectual property, property and contracts to the entity that should hold them, settle intercompany balances, and distribute or transfer cash. Intra-group transfers have tax consequences and this is where your accountant earns their fee
  5. Choose the route per entity.Strike-off or MVL, decided on the numbers rather than by default
  6. Execute and close.Strike-off applications, or an MVL run by a licensed practitioner. A strike-off cannot be applied for if the company has traded, changed its name or disposed of trading property in the previous three months, or while any insolvency or scheme process is live, so that three-month clock starts when the entity actually goes quiet rather than when you decide to close it. Then final tax returns, deregistrations, and closing the bank accounts
  7. Tidy what remains.Fewer entities, correct names, a structure chart someone can read

For a group of five to fifteen companies the whole exercise typically runs six to twelve months, governed less by the work than by tax clearances and the twelve-month period an MVL takes to reach dissolution. It is a project the finance function can run alongside its normal work rather than a service that takes the business over.

What to check before you close anything

Dissolution is difficult to undo and the assets of a dissolved company can pass to the Crown as ownerless property, so the diligence matters more than the closing.

Assets still in the company.
A bank account, a small shareholding, a registered trade mark, a domain name, a property title never transferred. These are the ones that surface afterwards
Guarantees and cross-guarantees.
Group borrowing frequently makes every company a guarantor. Closing one can breach the facility
Leases and dilapidations.
A lease held by a dormant company is a live liability until it is assigned or surrendered
Employment history.
Old employees, TUPE history, and any pension scheme the entity participated in
Litigation or claims
threatened or possible, including warranties given on an earlier sale
Whether it is really dormant.
Dormant means no significant accounting transaction in the year, meaning one that has to go into the accounting records, and a single one is enough to break it. The confirmation statement fee and a handful of other registrar fees are specifically disregarded, which is why entities get wrongly assumed dormant in both directions. Where an entity turns out to be insolvent rather than dormant, the insolvency practitioner page explains what changes

A company can be restored to the register after dissolution, but it is a court process. The cheaper administrative route exists only where the registrar struck the company off, not where you applied to strike it off yourself, so for a voluntary strike-off the court is the only way back. It costs considerably more than the check that would have avoided it.

Why it is worth doing before a sale

On any transaction, due diligence works through every company in the group. Dormant companies generate enquiries, and enquiries generate warranties, indemnities, retentions and delay. A group structure with four clean companies is a materially easier deal than the same business held through fourteen, and the difference shows up in the timetable, the legal fees and occasionally the price.

It is also work that cannot be done at the last minute. An MVL takes months, and a buyer will not wait for a tidy-up to complete. Two years before an intended exit is the right time, which is why this sits alongside exit planning in the same restructuring section. If you are not sure the group is ready, an independent business review establishes the position first.
