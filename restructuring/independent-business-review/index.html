Independent business review

An independent business review, universally shortened to IBR, is an objective assessment of whether a business is viable and what its realistic prospects are. It is usually commissioned by a lender, normally paid for by the borrower, and produced by a firm that reports to the lender. If yours has just asked for one, that combination is the first thing to get straight, because it explains everything about how the process feels.

Why your lender has asked for one

Something has changed in how the bank sees the account. Common triggers, in rough order of frequency:

A covenant breach
or a forecast that shows one coming
A facility approaching renewal
where the numbers no longer support automatic rollover
Late or unreliable information.
Management accounts that arrive months in arrears, or that do not reconcile to the statutory accounts, generate more anxiety than bad numbers delivered on time
A material change in performance
: a loss, a big customer gone, margins visibly compressing
A request for more money
particularly if the last projection was missed
A move to the bank's business support or restructuring team
which almost always precedes a request for a review
Sector risk
where the lender is reviewing a whole book rather than singling you out. That is the moment a contingency planning exercise earns its keep, because you can see it coming

The trigger matters, because it tells you what the reviewer will be asked to focus on. A lender worried about a covenant wants to know about headroom and forecast reliability. One worried about a sector wants to know how exposed you are relative to peers.

What an IBR actually looks at

Scope varies with the lender's concern, but the core is consistent.

The current financial position
verified rather than accepted. Real cash, real debtors, real creditors, real stock, and what the balance sheet does not show
Short-term cash.
A thirteen-week cash forecast is the standard instrument, and the reviewer will test its assumptions rather than take them
The integrity of the forecasts.
Not whether they are optimistic, which they usually are, but whether the assumptions behind them are defensible and whether previous forecasts were met
The business model and market.
Where the profit comes from by product, customer and contract, and whether that is durable
Management.
Whether the team has the information, the capability and the willingness to act. Reviewers form a view on this and it carries real weight
The funding structure.
What is owed to whom, on what security, and whether the facilities fit the business
Options.
What could be done: turnaround measures, debt restructuring, asset sales, new equity, or a formal procedure
Downside.
What the lender would recover in an insolvency, usually with an estimated outcome statement comparing scenarios

That last item is the one directors find hardest to see in a document about their own business. It is standard, it is in every IBR, and its presence does not mean anyone has decided anything.

Who sees it, and who pays

The lender commissions it and is the reviewer's client. The borrower almost always pays, under the facility terms that oblige the company to meet the lender's professional costs. The report goes to the lender. You will usually see it, and you should always ask to, but you are not entitled to it as of right unless the engagement says so.

That arrangement feels wrong the first time. It is standard across the industry and it is not a trap: the reviewer's value to the lender depends entirely on being independent, which means they are not simply writing what the bank wants to hear. A reviewer who produces an unbalanced report is worth nothing to the bank the next time.

What you can influence is the scope. Before it starts, ask what the terms of reference are, whether you will receive the report, and whether you will have the chance to comment on factual accuracy before it is issued. Reasonable lenders agree to all three.

How to come out of one well

This is the part no other page on the subject covers, and it makes a material difference.

Cooperate fully and quickly.
Delay and reluctance are read as concealment, and the reviewer will say so in the report. Nothing costs a director more credibility
Get your own numbers straight first.
If you can, produce the thirteen-week cash forecast before the reviewer asks. Arriving with it says more about management quality than anything you can assert
Do not present the optimistic case.
Reviewers test forecasts for a living and an unrealistic one destroys trust in everything else you say. A conservative forecast that is then beaten is worth far more than an ambitious one that is missed
Have your own plan.
A director who can set out what they intend to do, with numbers attached, changes the report. A director waiting to be told what to do also changes it
Bring your own adviser.
You are entitled to advice on your side of this. Somebody who has written IBRs knows what the reviewer is testing and what a good answer looks like
Do not hide the bad item.
It will be found, and being found is much worse than being told

The consistent pattern: reviewers are not looking for a perfect business. They are looking for management that understands its own position and is acting on it. That is what they report on, and it is largely within your control even when the numbers are not.

What happens afterwards

The lender takes a decision, and the realistic range is narrower than most directors fear.

  1. Continued support, unchanged.Common where the review shows a viable business with a temporary problem
  2. Support with conditions.Tighter covenants, more frequent reporting, a monitoring role for the reviewer, or specific actions with deadlines. This is the most common outcome
  3. A restructuring of the facilities.Reprofiled repayments, a new term, sometimes additional security. Our debt restructuring page covers what lenders typically agree to
  4. A requirement to raise money or sell something.New equity, an asset disposal, or a sale of the business, usually with a timetable attached
  5. Withdrawal of support.The rarest outcome, and one lenders reach reluctantly because enforcing usually recovers less than waiting. Where it happens, our administration and liquidation pages set out what follows

A properly conducted IBR frequently helps the borrower, because it replaces the lender's anxiety with information. Businesses with a real problem and credible management come out of reviews with more time more often than not.

Pre-lend reviews, and commissioning one yourself

The same discipline is used at two other moments.

A pre-lend review is commissioned before a lender advances money rather than after it worries about it: the same testing of forecasts, model and management, applied to a proposal. Borrowers who go through one and are approved get better terms than they would have had, because the lender is pricing less uncertainty.

And a director can commission a review for their own purposes, which is the version we most often recommend. Where you suspect a problem but are not sure of its size, an independent assessment gives you the facts before your lender asks for them, on your terms and with the report addressed to you. It is also the natural first step if you are considering an exit, since it establishes what a buyer's adviser will find. Our exit planning page picks that up.
