Contingency planning

A contingency plan is the answer to a question most directors avoid asking: what do we do if this does not work? If the refinancing is declined, if the contract is not renewed, if the biggest customer goes elsewhere or goes under. Not a forecast, and not a prediction that any of it will happen. A worked-through answer, prepared while there is still time to choose between options rather than take the only one left.

Why the timing decides the outcome

The uncomfortable arithmetic of financial distress is that your options narrow as your position weakens, and they narrow faster than most directors expect.

A company with six months of runway can sell a division, refinance, negotiate with a landlord, restructure its debt, run a proper sale process, or decide to close in an orderly way and pay its creditors. The same company with six weeks can do almost none of that. The buyer knows you are forced. The lender has already made its decision. The sale that would have realised full value becomes a fire sale, and a solvent closure becomes an insolvent one.

Nothing about the business changed in between. Only the time did. That is the entire case for doing this work early, and it is why our restructuring page argues that timing matters more than technique.

When to build one

The trigger is not distress. It is a foreseeable event with a real chance of going the wrong way, and a consequence you have not worked through.

A refinancing or facility renewal that is not certain.
The single most common one. If the answer is no, what happens on the Monday after?
A major contract, tender or customer up for renewal
where losing it would take the business below the line
A lender that has started asking different questions
moved you to a different team, or commissioned an independent business review
A large, uncertain liability
: an HMRC enquiry, a dispute, a dilapidations claim, a warranty exposure from an earlier sale
A sector or supply chain under visible strain
where you can see what is coming towards you before it arrives
A shareholder or partner dispute
that could force a sale or a split
A personal deadline
such as a guarantee falling due, retirement, or ill health
If any of those is on your horizon and you have not written down what you would do, the plan is worth an afternoon.

What a contingency plan actually contains

It is a short, practical document, not a consultancy report. Ours generally cover six things.

The trigger points.
What specific event or number means the fallback is now live. A cash balance, a date, a decision from a third party. Written down in advance, because the hardest part of any plan is admitting the moment has arrived
The real cash position, forward.
A short-term cash forecast that survives contact with reality, showing the point at which the company runs out of room. Most plans fail here, on optimistic debtor collection
The options, priced and ranked.
Sale, refinancing, a turnaround programme, a company voluntary arrangement, administration, a pre-pack administration, or an orderly liquidation. What each would realistically achieve, what it costs, how long it takes, and what has to be true for it to work
The preparation each option needs.
Some routes require weeks of groundwork: a buyer identified, a valuation done, funding lined up, information ready for a purchaser's adviser. Doing that work in advance is what makes the option real rather than theoretical
Your personal exposure, mapped.
Every personal guarantee, the director's loan account, anything you have secured on your own property. This is the part directors most often discover too late, and it frequently changes which option they choose
Who does what, and who is told.
Advisers, lenders, key staff, and the order in which they hear anything

The output is usually a handful of pages. Its value is not the document. It is that the thinking is done, so that the day it is needed you are executing a decision rather than making one.

What it is not

Two things this page is regularly confused with, and neither is what we do here.

It is not business continuity planning

Continuity and disaster-recovery planning deals with fires, floods, cyber incidents and IT failure: how the business keeps operating through a disruption. Useful, and someone else's speciality. This page is about financial contingency, where the risk is solvency rather than operations.

It is not a decision to do anything

Building a plan commits you to nothing. Most of the plans we write are never executed, because the refinancing lands or the contract renews. That is a good outcome and the work still earned its place: you spent the intervening months making decisions with the downside understood rather than guessed at.

Directors' duties, and why a plan protects you

Once a company is unable to pay its debts, or close to it, a director's duties shift towards the interests of creditors, and the decisions taken in that period are the ones examined later if things do not recover. Wrongful trading is not about a company failing. It is about what you knew, when you knew it, and what you did next.

A director who identified the risk early, took advice, documented the options and acted on a plan is in a materially stronger position than one who pressed on and hoped. That holds whether or not the plan was ever needed. The paper trail a contingency plan creates is one of the better protections a director can give themselves, and it costs a fraction of what defending the alternative costs.

How we run it

It is deliberately light. A first conversation to understand the business and what is coming. A short piece of work on the numbers and the options, usually one to three weeks depending on complexity and how good the information is. Then a document, a conversation about it, and a review point tied to whatever the trigger event is.

Where the review shows the business needs attention now rather than a plan for later, we say so, and the work becomes a turnaround or a debt restructuring conversation instead. Where it shows the company is fine, we say that too, and you have spent very little to find out.

Where we fit

Lancefield takes no insolvency appointments. Nobody here becomes your administrator or your liquidator, which means we have no financial interest in your contingency plan ever being executed, and no reason to steer you towards the formal option that would generate a fee for the firm.

That is the whole reason this service can be honest. An adviser who takes appointments and tells you to prepare for administration is describing work they might later be paid for. We are not, so when we tell you the fallback should be a solvent sale, or that you do not need a plan at all, you can take it at face value.

It is also why accountants and solicitors send us clients at this stage rather than later: they know the client will come back to them, not be absorbed into a process.
